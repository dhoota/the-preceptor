# Textbook citation pass: s26, s29, s50, s53

Label: s26-s29-s50-s53. Scope: every question in these four files that cited Rosen's Emergency Medicine or Goldfrank's Toxicologic Emergencies. That is 5 Rosen questions in s26 (burns), 11 Rosen questions in s29 (msk), 7 Rosen questions in s50 (shock) and 28 Goldfrank questions in s53 (tox), for 51 in all.

The brief for this pass listed 10 Rosen questions in s50. The file has 7. They are shock-26 q1, q3 and q4, shock-27 q3, shock-31 q3, shock-46 q1 and shock-47 q1. No other question in s50 cites Rosen, Goldfrank or another textbook from the brief. tox-47 q2 cites Rosenson 2013, a phenobarbital trial, and is not a textbook citation.

The content from commit 217fde7 is kept. No stem, option, key or keyFeature mapping changed. Every SAMP stays reviewed: false. The generic "rosen" and "goldfrank" source objects are gone from all four files.

Every guideline and study cited below was opened and read, in full text or through the body's own page, PMC or the Europe PMC abstract. The AHA 2023 poisoning update was read in full from a PDF copy of the Circulation article. The existing AHA source object in s53 had no link, so its DOI was added.

## How the chapters were checked

- Rosen's, 10th edition, 2023. Chapter numbers and titles come from the Elsevier table of contents (https://www.us.elsevierhealth.com/rosens-emergency-medicine-concepts-and-clinical-practice-9780323757898.html). The editors are Walls, Hockberger, Gausche-Hill, Erickson and Wilcox. Chapters used: 43 Wrist and Forearm Injuries, 44 Humerus and Elbow Injuries, 49 Ankle and Foot Injuries, 54 Thermal Injuries, 80 Gastroenteritis, 142 Cardiovascular Drugs. The chapter text itself was not available, so each chapter was chosen by its title.
- Goldfrank's, 11th edition, 2019. Chapter 3, Initial Evaluation of the Patient: Vital Signs and Toxic Syndromes, and Chapter 4, Principles of Managing the Acutely Poisoned or Overdosed Patient, come from the McGraw Hill product page table of contents (https://www.mheducation.com/highered/mhp/product/goldfrank-s-toxicologic-emergencies-eleventh-edition.html). That list stops after Chapter 8.
- Eight other Goldfrank's chapters are cited by title and author with no number. The title, author and address of each were found on AccessEmergency Medicine, but the full table of contents needs a login and returned 403, and the archived copies load the list by script. Search snippets gave conflicting numbers (Antipsychotics as 67 or 69, Nicotine as 84 while the platform's own chapter 84 address is Aluminum), so no number was guessed. The chapters are Toxic Alcohols (Wiener SW), Antiepileptics (Doyon S), Methylxanthines and Selective β2-Adrenergic Agonists (Hoffman RJ), Methemoglobin Inducers (Price DP), Antipsychotics (Juurlink DN), Amphetamines (Spyres MB, Jang DH), Fluid, Electrolyte, and Acid-Base Principles (Charney AN, Hoffman RS) and Nicotine (Fernández D, Soghoian S). The publisher's title joins Acid and Base with a longer dash. The citation uses a hyphen for house style.

## For the physician

- tox-42 q1. The key is 100 mL of 3% saline. The Society for Endocrinology guidance gives its bolus volume in a figure that could not be read as text. Summaries of the European 2014 guideline give 150 mL, and the US expert panel 2013 is usually quoted as 100 mL. I could open neither full text. The key is the only hypertonic option, so it stands either way. Please confirm the volume you want shown.
- tox-24 q3. Spiller 2005 found no coma, respiratory depression or hypotension below 0.3 mg in children, and coma with respiratory depression from 0.3 mg (0.015 mg/kg). This toddler took 0.2 mg. "Slow breathing with bradycardia" is still the best option, but you may want to judge whether the stem dose fits the tested complication.
- shock-26 q3. The phenytoin line ("can worsen conduction in a sodium channel blocking overdose") rests on Rosen's. Emergency Care BC says to avoid phenytoin in TCA overdose. AHA 2023 lists phenytoin among class Ib agents proposed for class Ia and Ic cardiotoxicity. The key, lorazepam, is not in doubt.

### tox-40 q1 in detail

AHA 2023 (read from a PDF copy of the Circulation article) recommends sedation for severe agitation (class 1) and gives benzodiazepines, antipsychotics and ketamine as examples. It separately recommends rapid external cooling for life threatening hyperthermia (class 1) and calls prolonged restraint without sedation potentially harmful. It also says adequate sedation generally obviates antihypertensives.

The prompt asks for the most important initial treatment in a man at 40.4°C. The key, active cooling with sedation, meets both class 1 recommendations. Haloperidol 5 mg IM alone meets only the sedation one and leaves the temperature untreated, so it is still wrong because of the word "alone". It is not a second defensible answer. No key or option change.

The writer's sentence "Haloperidol alone may calm him but does nothing to cool him" overstated the case. AHA says sedatives reduce the agitation that produces heat, so sedation does lower heat production. The sentence now reads "Haloperidol alone may control the agitation, but it leaves a temperature of 40.4°C without the rapid external cooling he needs." The propranolol reason is supported word for word by AHA and stays.

### Flags

- tox-42 q1, 100 mL volume.
