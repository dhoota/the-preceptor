## Keys or explanations changed

No key changed. These explanations changed:

- multiple-trauma-23 q4. Old: an ETCO2 rise is "an early and reliable sign that circulation has returned, and prompts a pulse check". New: it is "the best of these signs that circulation may have returned", it is not enough on its own to stop compressions, and it is combined with other signs before the next rhythm and pulse check. ERC 2025 says a rise "may indicate" ROSC and that compressions should not stop on this sign alone.
- multiple-trauma-30 q2. Old: "Corticosteroids have not been shown to improve outcome once the syndrome is established." New: "Corticosteroids have inconsistent benefit and possible harm, so they are not standard treatment." This matches the Miyake review.
- multiple-trauma-34 q2. The claims that a soft dressing lets fragments "bleed" and that an unsplinted leg causes "blood loss" were removed. NAEMSP 2025 states hemorrhagic shock from femoral shaft fractures is uncommon.
- multiple-trauma-44 q1. Old: potassium "floods into the circulation". New: potassium "can enter the circulation quickly", and severe hyperkalemia is uncommon after crush injury but can cause arrhythmia and arrest within minutes. The FPHC consensus finds clinically significant hyperkalemia uncommon, even after prolonged entrapment.
- multiple-trauma-44 q2. Old: "An oral potassium binder acts too slowly." New: "Sodium polystyrene sulfonate acts too slowly to help in the next few minutes." ERC 2025 recommends sodium zirconium cyclosilicate 10 g orally for potassium removal, so a blanket statement about binders was wrong.

### multiple-trauma-44 q1 in detail

The key (hyperkalemic arrhythmia) stays defensible because the question asks for the most immediate threat, not the most likely one, and the options leave no rival that kills in minutes. Acute kidney injury develops over hours to days. Compartment syndrome threatens the limb, not life, in the next minutes. Fat embolism has no fracture. Tetany is not a lethal early event. Hypovolemic shock, the likeliest rival, is not an option. He is 30 minutes from release, which is the window of concern, and q2 then confirms potassium 6.8 with a wide QRS.

The weakness was in the explanation. After the writer's change it said the keyed complication is uncommon but never said why it is still the answer. I added one sentence: "Of these options it is the only one that can kill him in minutes, so it is looked for first with an ECG and a blood gas." This fits FPHC recommendation 10 (interval cardiac monitoring and point of care potassium). For the physician: the key is weak in the sense that an examiner following FPHC could argue it tests a de-emphasized concern. It is not wrong.
