### eye-16: Newborn with swollen eyelids

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 4-day-old boy is brought to the emergency department by his mother with 12 hours of swelling of both eyelids and thick yellow discharge from both eyes. He was born at 37 weeks by vaginal delivery after a pregnancy with no prenatal care. No ocular prophylaxis was given at birth. He is feeding well and has had six wet diapers today. His mother mentions a new vaginal discharge since the delivery. Both sets of lids are tense and swollen. Pus reaccumulates within a minute of being wiped away. The conjunctivae are red and swollen.

- Temperature: 37.1°C rectal
- Pulse: 150/minute
- Resp.: 42/minute
- O2 sat: 98% on room air
- Weight: 2.5 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most likely cause of the eye findings in this newborn?**

- A. Chemical conjunctivitis
- B. Chlamydia trachomatis
- C. Herpes simplex virus
- D. Nasolacrimal duct obstruction
- E. Neisseria gonorrhoeae  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Copious pus that reaccumulates within a minute, tense lid swelling and onset on day 4 after an unscreened pregnancy point to gonococcal ophthalmia. The new maternal discharge adds weight. Chlamydial conjunctivitis usually starts between days 5 and 14 and is milder. Chemical conjunctivitis follows prophylaxis within a day, and none was given. Herpes simplex gives watery discharge, often with vesicles. Duct obstruction causes mucoid discharge without red, swollen conjunctivae.

*Key feature:* Eye: red eye and loss of vision 1. Before calling a red eye conjunctivitis, exclude serious causes such as glaucoma, iritis, keratitis, or a foreign body.

*Source:* Moore DL, MacDonald NE, Canadian Paediatric Society Infectious Diseases and Immunization Committee. Preventing ophthalmia neonatorum. Paediatr Child Health. 2015. https://academic.oup.com/pch/article/20/2/93/2647267

#### Q2
*Update:* A conjunctival Gram stain shows gram-negative intracellular diplococci. The lids have to be held open with retractors to see the globes.

**Which of the following parts of the eye examination is most important in this infant before admission?**

- A. Corneal fluorescein examination  **(key)**
- B. Dilated fundus examination
- C. Intraocular pressure measurement
- D. Red reflex with an ophthalmoscope
- E. Visual fixation and following

*Explanation:* Neisseria gonorrhoeae can invade intact corneal epithelium, and ulceration can progress to perforation within days. Fluorescein examination of each cornea finds early ulceration, which needs same day ophthalmology. The red reflex belongs to every newborn examination but does not assess this threat. A dilated fundus view and tonometry add nothing to a surface infection. Fixation and following are not yet reliable at 4 days of age.

*Key feature:* Eye: red eye and loss of vision 3. Record visual acuity for every eye complaint, then do a full eye exam including slit lamp.

*Source:* Cheung AY, Choi DS, Ahmad S, et al, American Academy of Ophthalmology Preferred Practice Pattern Cornea/External Disease Panel. Conjunctivitis Preferred Practice Pattern. Ophthalmology. 2024. https://www.aao.org/education/preferred-practice-pattern/conjunctivitis-ppp-2023

#### Q3
**Which of the following is the most appropriate antibiotic treatment for this infant?**

- A. azithromycin 50 mg PO daily for 3 days
- B. ceftriaxone 125 mg IM once  **(key)**
- C. ceftriaxone 250 mg IM once
- D. erythromycin 0.5% ointment to both eyes
- E. tobramycin 0.3% drops every hour

*Explanation:* Gonococcal ophthalmia needs systemic treatment. The Canadian Paediatric Society dose of ceftriaxone is 50 mg/kg to a maximum of 125 mg IV or IM as a single dose. At 2.5 kg that is 50 x 2.5 = 125 mg. A 250 mg dose is 100 mg/kg, twice the weight based dose, and exceeds the Canadian maximum. Topical erythromycin or tobramycin alone does not treat gonococcal infection. Azithromycin 20 mg/kg for 3 days is a regimen for chlamydial disease. An infant receiving IV calcium would get cefotaxime instead, because ceftriaxone is contraindicated with it.

*Key feature:* Eye: red eye and loss of vision 1. Before calling a red eye conjunctivitis, exclude serious causes such as glaucoma, iritis, keratitis, or a foreign body.

*Source:* Moore DL, MacDonald NE, Canadian Paediatric Society Infectious Diseases and Immunization Committee. Preventing ophthalmia neonatorum. Paediatr Child Health. 2015. https://academic.oup.com/pch/article/20/2/93/2647267

#### Q4
**Which of the following are appropriate additional steps in the care of this infant and his family?**

Select 4.

- A. Bilateral eye patching
- B. Chlamydia testing of the conjunctival swab  **(key)**
- C. Discharge with review in 24 hours
- D. Hospital admission  **(key)**
- E. Lacrimal sac massage
- F. Saline irrigation of both eyes  **(key)**
- G. Testing and treatment of both parents  **(key)**
- H. Topical corticosteroid drops

*Explanation:* An infant with gonococcal ophthalmia is admitted for treatment and monitoring for spread, such as sepsis, arthritis or meningitis. Frequent saline irrigation clears the pus that damages the cornea. Chlamydia can coexist and is tested for on the conjunctival specimen. The mother and her partner need testing and treatment, or reinfection follows. Patching traps pus against the cornea. Sac massage treats duct obstruction, which he does not have. Topical steroids have no role in this infection.

*Key feature:* Eye: red eye and loss of vision 2. Take a detailed eye history, including onset, trauma, pain, and vision change, plus related systemic disease like temporal arteritis.

*Source:* Moore DL, MacDonald NE, Canadian Paediatric Society Infectious Diseases and Immunization Committee. Preventing ophthalmia neonatorum. Paediatr Child Health. 2015. https://academic.oup.com/pch/article/20/2/93/2647267

### eye-17: Both eyes painful after a day outdoors

A 26-year-old woman presents to the emergency department at 01:30 with severe pain in both eyes that woke her an hour ago. Both eyes water and feel gritty, and she cannot keep them open in the light. Yesterday she spent 6 hours spring skiing at high altitude on a clear, sunny day. She lost her goggles early in the morning. She does not wear contact lenses and has no history of eye disease. She takes no medications. Both eyes are red and she is holding them shut with her hands.

- Temperature: 36.7°C oral
- Pulse: 92/minute
- Resp.: 16/minute
- BP: 128/78 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most appropriate first step in the examination of her eyes?**

- A. Anesthetic drops, then visual acuity  **(key)**
- B. Fluorescein staining of both corneas
- C. Intraocular pressure in both eyes
- D. Pupil examination with a penlight
- E. Upper lid eversion in both eyes

*Explanation:* Visual acuity is recorded first for every eye complaint, and she cannot open her eyes because of pain and spasm. A drop of topical anesthetic in each eye lets her cooperate with acuity testing and the rest of the examination. Pupils, lid eversion, pressure and fluorescein staining all follow. Starting with them leaves no baseline acuity if the course later changes. A topical anesthetic is suitable here because nothing suggests a penetrating injury, but it is never sent home with her.

*Key feature:* Eye: red eye and loss of vision 3. Record visual acuity for every eye complaint, then do a full eye exam including slit lamp.

*Source:* Wipperman JL, Dorsch JN. Evaluation and management of corneal abrasions. Am Fam Physician. 2013. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23317075/

#### Q2
*Update:* After tetracaine 0.5%, visual acuity is 20/30 in each eye. Pupils are equal and reactive. Fluorescein shows fine punctate staining in a horizontal band across both corneas. Lid eversion shows no foreign body. The anterior chambers are quiet.

**Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis in this patient?**

- A. Adenoviral keratoconjunctivitis
- B. Allergic conjunctivitis
- C. Bilateral corneal abrasions
- D. Herpes simplex keratitis
- E. Ultraviolet keratitis  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Punctate staining in the exposed band of both corneas, with pain starting hours after long sun exposure on snow without goggles, is ultraviolet keratitis. Symptoms typically begin 6 to 12 hours after exposure. Adenovirus usually starts in one eye with discharge and a tender preauricular node. Allergy causes itch rather than severe pain. Abrasions follow a scratch and stain as a defined defect. Herpes simplex keratitis is usually one sided with a branching dendrite.

*Key feature:* Eye: red eye and loss of vision 3. Record visual acuity for every eye complaint, then do a full eye exam including slit lamp.

*Source:* The College of Optometrists. Photokeratitis (ultraviolet [UV] burn, arc eye, snow blindness). Clinical Management Guidelines. 2025. https://www.college-optometrists.org/clinical-guidance/clinical-management-guidelines/photokeratitis_ultraviolet_uv_burn_arceye_snowblin

#### Q3
**Which of the following findings would most suggest a diagnosis other than ultraviolet keratitis in this patient?**

- A. Bilateral lid spasm
- B. Conjunctival injection
- C. Focal corneal infiltrate  **(key)**
- D. Punctate corneal staining
- E. Tearing in both eyes

*Explanation:* A focal white corneal infiltrate means infection or inflammation of the corneal stroma, such as microbial keratitis, and needs same day ophthalmology. Ultraviolet injury damages only the epithelium, so it heals within 24 to 72 hours without an infiltrate. Lid spasm, tearing, conjunctival redness and punctate staining are all expected with ultraviolet keratitis. None of them points away from it.

*Key feature:* Eye: red eye and loss of vision 1. Before calling a red eye conjunctivitis, exclude serious causes such as glaucoma, iritis, keratitis, or a foreign body.

*Source:* American Academy of Ophthalmology Preferred Practice Pattern Cornea/External Disease Panel. Bacterial Keratitis Preferred Practice Pattern. Ophthalmology. 2024. https://www.aao.org/education/preferred-practice-pattern/bacterial-keratitis-ppp-2023

#### Q4
**Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment plan to discharge her with?**

- A. Double eye patches for 24 hours
- B. Ketorolac drops with pressure patching
- C. Oral analgesia and erythromycin ointment  **(key)**
- D. Prednisolone acetate drops four times daily
- E. Tetracaine drops to use at home

*Explanation:* Oral analgesia and a lubricating antibiotic ointment such as erythromycin keep her comfortable while the epithelium heals over 1 to 3 days. Repeated topical anesthetic delays epithelial healing and masks worsening, so it is not sent home. Patching does not speed healing of epithelial defects and would leave her unable to see. Topical steroids slow healing and risk infection. She should return if pain is not improving after 24 hours.

*Key feature:* Eye: red eye and loss of vision 1. Before calling a red eye conjunctivitis, exclude serious causes such as glaucoma, iritis, keratitis, or a foreign body.

### eye-19: Blurred distance vision and headache

A 31-year-old woman presents to the emergency department with blurred vision in both eyes and a frontal headache that began on waking this morning. Distant objects look blurred. She vomited once. She has had migraine with aura since age 15. Her neurologist started topiramate 25 mg daily 9 days ago and raised it to 50 mg daily 3 days ago. She takes no other medications. She has had normal distance vision all her life. Both eyes are red.

- Temperature: 36.9°C oral
- Pulse: 98/minute
- Resp.: 18/minute
- BP: 142/88 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air

#### Q1
*Update:* Visual acuity is 20/200 in each eye. Both pupils are 5 mm and react sluggishly. Both corneas are hazy and both anterior chambers are shallow. Intraocular pressure is 48 mmHg on the right and 46 mmHg on the left.

**Which of the following details of her history is most relevant to these eye findings?**

- A. Lifelong normal distance vision
- B. Migraine with aura since age 15
- C. Morning onset of symptoms
- D. Recent start of topiramate  **(key)**
- E. Vomiting before arrival

*Explanation:* Bilateral angle closure with a sudden myopic shift within 2 weeks of starting topiramate is the typical drug reaction, and in the largest case series 85% of cases began within the first 2 weeks. Lifelong normal distance vision makes the new blur a drug induced myopic shift rather than a long standing refractive error. Migraine is her indication for the drug, not the cause. Vomiting and morning onset occur with any cause of raised pressure.

*Key feature:* Eye: red eye and loss of vision 2. Take a detailed eye history, including onset, trauma, pain, and vision change, plus related systemic disease like temporal arteritis.

*Source:* Fraunfelder FW, Fraunfelder FT, Keates EU. Topiramate-associated acute, bilateral, secondary angle-closure glaucoma. Ophthalmology. 2004. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14711721/

#### Q2
**Which of the following eye drops are appropriate to start now for both of her eyes?**

Select 3.

- A. brimonidine 0.2% one drop  **(key)**
- B. cyclopentolate 1% one drop  **(key)**
- C. ketorolac 0.5% one drop
- D. moxifloxacin 0.5% one drop
- E. olopatadine 0.1% one drop
- F. pilocarpine 2% one drop
- G. tetracaine 0.5% one drop
- H. timolol 0.5% one drop  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Topiramate causes swelling of the ciliary body with choroidal effusion that pushes the lens and iris forward, so this is not pupil block. Pilocarpine contracts the ciliary muscle and can worsen the closure. Ketorolac, moxifloxacin, olopatadine and tetracaine do not treat the pressure.

*Source:* Fraunfelder FW, Fraunfelder FT, Keates EU. Topiramate-associated acute, bilateral, secondary angle-closure glaucoma. Ophthalmology. 2004. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14711721/

#### Q3
**Which of the following is the most important step in the definitive management of this patient?**

- A. Bilateral laser peripheral iridotomy
- B. Discontinuation of topiramate  **(key)**
- C. Pilocarpine 2% in both eyes
- D. Prednisone 50 mg PO daily
- E. Topiramate dose reduction to 25 mg

*Explanation:* Stopping topiramate is the key step, and the effusion and angle closure usually resolve within days once it is stopped, under ophthalmology care.
