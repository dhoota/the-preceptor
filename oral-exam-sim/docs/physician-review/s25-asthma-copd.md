### asthma-copd-16: A baby with a cough and noisy breathing

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 5-month-old boy is brought to the emergency department by his parents with three days of runny nose and cough and one day of noisy breathing. He is taking about half his usual feeds. He was born at 39 weeks and has been well. His 3-year-old sister has a cold. There has been no choking episode and the cough is not paroxysmal. He has never wheezed before. On examination he has nasal congestion, subcostal and intercostal retractions, and fine crackles with expiratory wheeze throughout both lung fields. Capillary refill is 2 seconds.

- Temperature: 37.9°C rectal
- Pulse: 158/minute
- Resp.: 56/minute
- O2 sat: 94% on room air
- Weight: 7.1 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis for this infant?**

- A. Bronchiolitis  **(key)**
- B. First asthma exacerbation
- C. Inhaled foreign body
- D. Pertussis infection
- E. Pneumococcal pneumonia

*Explanation:* A first episode of wheeze with diffuse crackles after two to three days of coryza, in an infant under 12 months with a sick contact, is bronchiolitis. Asthma should not be labelled on a first viral wheeze at 5 months. The findings are bilateral and there was no choking episode, which argues against an inhaled foreign body. Pneumococcal or other bacterial pneumonia is less likely with a temperature of 37.9°C and diffuse rather than focal findings. Pertussis is not suggested because the cough is not paroxysmal.

*Key feature:* Asthma/COPD 1. For a first wheezing episode, consider mimics such as foreign body, heart failure, PE, or anaphylaxis before labelling it asthma.

*Source:* Friedman JN, Rieder MJ, Walton JM, Canadian Paediatric Society. Bronchiolitis. Recommendations for diagnosis, monitoring and management of children one to 24 months of age. Paediatr Child Health. 2014, updated 2021. https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/bronchiolitis

#### Q2
**Which of the following is the most appropriate initial treatment for this infant?**

- A. dexamethasone 4.3 mg PO
- B. Nebulized epinephrine
- C. Nebulized hypertonic saline
- D. salbutamol by MDI and spacer
- E. Superficial nasal suctioning  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Bronchiolitis care is supportive, and his nasal congestion is limiting feeds, so superficial nasal suctioning is the appropriate first step. The Canadian Paediatric Society advises that suctioning, if done, be superficial rather than deep. It recommends against routine salbutamol, epinephrine and corticosteroids. Salbutamol does not improve oxygen saturation or admission rates, and corticosteroids give no meaningful improvement in outcomes or hospitalization. Hypertonic 3% saline is also not supported in routine cases. Treating this infant as asthma would add drugs without benefit.

*Key feature:* Asthma/COPD 1. For a first wheezing episode, consider mimics such as foreign body, heart failure, PE, or anaphylaxis before labelling it asthma.

*Source:* Friedman JN, Rieder MJ, Walton JM, Canadian Paediatric Society. Bronchiolitis. Recommendations for diagnosis, monitoring and management of children one to 24 months of age. Paediatr Child Health. 2014, updated 2021. https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/bronchiolitis

### asthma-copd-17: Noisy breathing at the regatta

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 16-year-old girl is brought to the emergency department from a rowing regatta with throat tightness and noisy breathing. She has had four similar episodes this season, each labelled as exercise-induced asthma. Salbutamol taken before races has not prevented them. She has no food allergy, took no new medication and was not stung. She takes no other medications. She speaks in full sentences and holds her hand to her throat. There is a harsh inspiratory sound heard loudest over the larynx. The chest is clear with no expiratory wheeze. There is no urticaria or lip swelling.

- Pulse: 108/minute
- Resp.: 24/minute
- BP: 122/74 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most likely cause of her current symptoms?**

- A. Anaphylaxis to an unknown trigger
- B. Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction
- C. Inducible laryngeal obstruction  **(key)**
- D. Inhaled foreign body
- E. Spontaneous pneumothorax

*Explanation:* Inspiratory noise loudest over the larynx, a clear chest, normal SpO2, full sentences and recurrent episodes that salbutamol did not prevent fit inducible laryngeal obstruction. Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction causes expiratory wheeze in the chest, which she does not have. There is no urticaria, lip swelling or trigger to suggest anaphylaxis. Recurrent stereotyped episodes argue against an inhaled foreign body. A pneumothorax would reduce breath sounds on one side rather than cause laryngeal noise.

*Key feature:* Asthma/COPD 1. For a first wheezing episode, consider mimics such as foreign body, heart failure, PE, or anaphylaxis before labelling it asthma.

*Source:* Halvorsen T, Walsted ES, Bucca C, et al. Inducible laryngeal obstruction. An official joint European Respiratory Society and European Laryngological Society statement. Eur Respir J. 2017. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28889105/

#### Q2
**Which of the following features of her episodes would most support an upper airway cause?**

- A. Chest tightness when training in cold air
- B. Cough at night during viral colds
- C. Eczema and allergic rhinitis in childhood
- D. Onset during maximal effort  **(key)**
- E. Symptoms peaking 10 minutes after exercise

*Explanation:* Onset during maximal effort points to the larynx. Exercise-induced laryngeal obstruction typically starts during high intensity exercise and settles within minutes of stopping, with noise on inspiration. Bronchoconstriction from exercise usually peaks after exercise has ended, so symptoms peaking 10 minutes afterward point to asthma. Night cough with colds, chest tightness in cold air and a history of atopy also support asthma rather than a laryngeal cause. Her failure to respond to salbutamol fits an upper airway cause.

*Key feature:* Asthma/COPD 1. For a first wheezing episode, consider mimics such as foreign body, heart failure, PE, or anaphylaxis before labelling it asthma.

*Source:* Halvorsen T, Walsted ES, Bucca C, et al. Inducible laryngeal obstruction. An official joint European Respiratory Society and European Laryngological Society statement. Eur Respir J. 2017. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28889105/

#### Q3
*Update:* Her symptoms settle over 20 minutes with reassurance and coached breathing. The examination is now normal.

**Which of the following investigations is most likely to confirm the diagnosis at follow-up?**

- A. Laryngoscopy during exercise  **(key)**
- B. Methacholine challenge test
- C. Neck CT at rest
- D. Serum tryptase level
- E. Spirometry with bronchodilator

*Explanation:* The joint ERS and ELS statement describes laryngoscopy during an episode, such as continuous laryngoscopy during exercise, as the reference test for exercise-induced laryngeal obstruction. A 52-year-old woman with severe asthma presents to the emergency department with two days of wheeze and breathlessness after a cold. - Pulse: 116/minute
- Resp.: 28/minute
- BP: 132/78 mmHg

#### Q1
**Which of the following features of her history most increase her risk of a fatal attack?**

Select 2.

- A. Age over 50 years
- B. Asthma admission last year  **(key)**
- C. Asthma onset in childhood
- D. Current daily prednisone use  **(key)**
- E. Use of ICS-formoterol reliever
- F. Viral trigger for this attack

*Explanation:* GINA lists hospitalization for asthma in the past year and current or recent use of oral corticosteroids among the red flags for fatal or near fatal asthma. She has both. *Key feature:* Asthma/COPD 2. In asthma or COPD flares, ask about prior ICU care or intubation, recent steroids, and frequent visits to gauge risk.

- A. 25%
- B. 40%  **(key)**
- C. 50%
- D. 60%
- E. 75%

*Explanation:* Her peak flow is 150 of 380 L/minute, which is 39.5%, closest to 40%. ### asthma-copd-47: A preschooler who spits out medicine

A 4-year-old boy is brought to the emergency department with a day of cough and wheeze after a cold. He has had three similar episodes in the past year and takes no daily medication. His mother has a salbutamol inhaler at home but no spacer. He is alert and talks in short sentences. There is suprasternal indrawing without scalene contraction. He has expiratory wheeze only, with decreased air entry at the bases.

- Temperature: 37.4°C tympanic
- Pulse: 136/minute
- Resp.: 34/minute
- O2 sat: 93% on room air
- Weight: 17 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following salbutamol doses should be repeated every 20 minutes for three doses?**

- A. salbutamol 5 mg by nebulizer
- B. salbutamol 10 mg by nebulizer
- C. salbutamol 2 puffs by MDI and spacer
- D. salbutamol 5 puffs by MDI and spacer  **(key)**
- E. salbutamol 10 puffs by MDI and spacer

*Explanation:* His PRAM score is 5 (suprasternal 2, scalene 0, expiratory wheeze 1, reduced air entry at the bases 1, SpO2 92 to 94% 1). TREKK doses salbutamol for a child under 20 kg at 5 puffs of 100 mcg by MDI and spacer, or 2.5 mg by nebulizer, every 20 minutes for three treatments. Ten puffs is the dose for 20 kg or more, and 2 puffs is too low. Nebulized doses of 5 mg and 10 mg exceed the 2.5 mg advised for his weight of 17 kg.

*Key feature:* Asthma/COPD 5. Start bronchodilators, steroids, and oxygen for acute asthma before the diagnosis is fully confirmed.

*Source:* TREKK. Pediatric severe asthma exacerbation algorithm for children aged 12 months and older. Version 2.0. 2024. https://cms.trekk.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/2024-02-19_Asthma-Algorithm_v2.0-FINAL.pdf

#### Q2
*Update:* His mother says he spat out prednisolone syrup during his last visit and vomited.
