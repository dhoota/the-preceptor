### gi-bleed-16: Black stools and bleeding gums

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 24-year-old woman presents to the emergency department with 2 days of black stools and light-headedness when she stands. For 5 days her gums have bled when she brushes her teeth, and her period, which began 6 days ago, is heavier than usual. She took ibuprofen 400 mg three times daily for 3 days for cramps. She had a cold 3 weeks ago. She takes no other medications, drinks alcohol rarely and has no liver disease. She is pale. There are blood blisters inside both cheeks and petechiae on both legs. There is no lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly. Rectal examination shows black tarry stool. Hemoglobin 88 g/L, WBC 6.8 x 10^9/L with a normal differential, platelets 5 x 10^9/L, INR 1.0, fibrinogen 3.1 g/L. The blood film shows few, large platelets with no blasts and no fragmented red cells.

- Temperature: 36.9°C oral
- Pulse: 112/minute
- Resp.: 18/minute
- BP: 102/64 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air
- Weight: 61 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following best explains why she is bleeding from several sites at once?**

- A. Acute myeloid leukemia
- B. Immune thrombocytopenia  **(key)**
- C. NSAID-induced peptic ulcer
- D. Thrombotic microangiopathy
- E. Von Willebrand disease

*Explanation:* An isolated platelet count of 5 x 10^9/L with large platelets on the film, a normal white count and a normal INR after a recent viral illness fits immune thrombocytopenia. It explains the gum bleeding, oral blood blisters, petechiae, heavy period and melena together. Leukemia is unlikely with a normal differential and no blasts. A thrombotic microangiopathy would show fragmented red cells. An NSAID ulcer could explain the melena but not the mucosal and skin bleeding. Von Willebrand disease does not cause a platelet count this low.

*Key feature:* Gastrointestinal (GI) bleed 1. In GI bleeding, look for clues to the source such as medications, alcohol, weight loss, liver disease, or coagulopathy.

*Source:* Provan D, Arnold DM, Bussel JB, et al. Updated international consensus report on the investigation and management of primary immune thrombocytopenia. Blood Adv. 2019. https://doi.org/10.1182/bloodadvances.2019000812

#### Q2
**Which of the following treatments should she receive now to raise her platelet count quickly?**

Select 2.

- A. desmopressin 0.3 mcg/kg IV
- B. immune globulin 1 g/kg IV  **(key)**
- C. methylprednisolone 1 g IV  **(key)**
- D. Plasma exchange
- E. rituximab 375 mg/m2 IV
- F. vitamin K 10 mg IV

*Explanation:* Melena with a hemoglobin of 88 g/L and a platelet count of 5 x 10^9/L is a life-threatening ITP bleed. The consensus report advises an IV corticosteroid combined with IV immune globulin when the count must rise within 24 hours. Rituximab takes 3 to 4 weeks to work, so it does not help now. Plasma exchange is listed as not indicated in ITP. Her INR is 1.0, so vitamin K adds nothing. Desmopressin is suggested only when reduced kidney function adds to bleeding, and her problem is platelet destruction.

**Which of the following blood products is most appropriate for her now?**

- B. Frozen plasma 15 mL/kg IV

*Explanation:* She is now in shock from ongoing bleeding with a platelet count of 5 x 10^9/L. Giving red cells alone leaves the cause of the bleeding untreated. Her fibrinogen is 3.1 g/L, so cryoprecipitate is not needed. Her INR is 1.0, so frozen plasma and prothrombin complex concentrate correct nothing.

CT angiography and a tagged red cell scan need active bleeding and cannot treat an ulcer. Capsule endoscopy is used for small bowel bleeding after normal upper and lower endoscopy.

### gi-bleed-17: Bleeding and drowsiness in a young infant

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 6-week-old boy is brought to the emergency department because of two stools streaked with bright red blood and new bruises on his chest. His parents say he has been sleepier than usual since this morning and fed poorly at his last two feeds. He was born at term at home, and his parents declined all newborn injections. He received no vitamin K by any route. He is exclusively breastfed. He has had no fever or vomiting. His mother takes no medications. He is pale and difficult to rouse. The anterior fontanelle is full. There are two bruises on the chest. The abdomen is soft.

- Temperature: 36.9°C rectal
- Pulse: 172/minute
- Resp.: 40/minute
- BP: 78/46 mmHg
- O2 sat: 98% on room air
- Weight: 4.6 kg

#### Q1
*Update:* INR 6.4, PTT 82 seconds (normal 25 to 38), platelets 356 x 10^9/L, fibrinogen 2.6 g/L, hemoglobin 88 g/L.

**Which of the following is the most likely cause of his bleeding?**

- A. Anal fissure from hard stools
- B. Cow's milk protein proctocolitis
- C. Immune thrombocytopenia
- D. Inherited factor VIII deficiency
- E. Vitamin K deficiency bleeding  **(key)**

*Explanation:* A breastfed infant who did not receive vitamin K at birth is at risk of late vitamin K deficiency bleeding, which typically presents between 2 and 12 weeks. His INR of 6.4 with a prolonged PTT, normal platelets and normal fibrinogen fits deficiency of the vitamin K dependent factors. Factor VIII deficiency prolongs the PTT but leaves the INR normal. Immune thrombocytopenia is excluded by a platelet count of 356 x 10^9/L. A fissure or proctocolitis would not explain bruising and a coagulopathy.

*Key feature:* Gastrointestinal (GI) bleed 1. In GI bleeding, look for clues to the source such as medications, alcohol, weight loss, liver disease, or coagulopathy.

*Source:* Ng E, Loewy AD. Guidelines for vitamin K prophylaxis in newborns. Canadian Paediatric Society and College of Family Physicians of Canada position statement. Paediatr Child Health. 2018. https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/vitamin-k-prophylaxis-in-newborns

#### Q2
**Which of the following investigations is most urgent for him now?**

- A. Abdominal ultrasound
- B. CT head without contrast  **(key)**
- C. Skeletal survey
- D. Stool culture
- E. Upper GI contrast study

*Explanation:* Drowsiness, poor feeding and a full fontanelle in an infant with a coagulopathy suggest intracranial hemorrhage, the most common serious site of late vitamin K deficiency bleeding. CT head without contrast is the most urgent test. A skeletal survey belongs in the assessment of unexplained bruising but does not change the next hour of care. Abdominal ultrasound, stool culture and a contrast study do not address the threat to his brain.

*Key feature:* Gastrointestinal (GI) bleed 1. In GI bleeding, look for clues to the source such as medications, alcohol, weight loss, liver disease, or coagulopathy.

*Source:* Araki S, Shirahata A. Vitamin K deficiency bleeding in infancy. Nutrients. 2020. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7146284/

#### Q3
**Which of the following treatments are most appropriate to correct his coagulopathy now?**

Select 2.

- A. Cryoprecipitate 5 mL/kg IV
- B. Desmopressin 0.3 mcg/kg IV
- C. Frozen plasma 15 mL/kg IV  **(key)**
- D. Platelets 10 mL/kg IV
- E. Tranexamic acid 15 mg/kg IV
- F. Vitamin K 1 mg IV  **(key)**
- G. Vitamin K 1 mg PO

*Explanation:* Vitamin K given IV starts to correct the deficiency within hours, and the IV route is used because he may be bleeding intracranially. Frozen plasma, or prothrombin complex concentrate where local protocols allow, replaces the missing factors at once while vitamin K takes effect. Oral vitamin K is too slow and less reliable for active serious bleeding. His platelets and fibrinogen are normal, so platelets and cryoprecipitate do not help. Desmopressin and tranexamic acid do not correct a vitamin K deficiency.

#### Q4
*Update:* CT head shows a small subdural hemorrhage, and neurosurgery is consulted. His parents ask how this could be prevented in a future baby.

**Which of the following is the most effective way to prevent this in their future newborns?**

- A. IM vitamin K at birth  **(key)**
- B. Maternal vitamin K supplements
- C. Oral vitamin K once at birth
- D. Routine newborn coagulation tests
- E. Switching to formula feeding

*Explanation:* The Canadian Paediatric Society recommends a single IM dose of vitamin K, 0.5 to 1.0 mg, for every newborn, and this reliably prevents vitamin K deficiency bleeding. Coagulation tests do not prevent bleeding. Formula feeding is not needed when the infant receives IM vitamin K, and breastfeeding should be supported.

Last week they found a television remote control open with its battery missing. He has no past medical history and takes no medications. He is alert, pale and crying. The nose and oropharynx show no bleeding. The chest is clear and the abdomen is soft.

- Temperature: 37.9°C rectal
- Pulse: 150/minute
- Resp.: 30/minute
- BP: 90/56 mmHg
- O2 sat: 97% on room air
- Weight: 12 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most likely cause of his hematemesis?**

- A. Erosive esophagitis from reflux
- B. Esophageal button battery injury  **(key)**
- C. Mallory Weiss tear from vomiting
- D. Peptic ulcer disease
- E. Swallowed blood from epistaxis

*Explanation:* Six days of drooling and refusal of solids after a battery went missing suggest an impacted esophageal button battery.
