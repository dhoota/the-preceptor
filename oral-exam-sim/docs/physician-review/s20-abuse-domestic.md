### abuse-domestic-16: School-aged boy with a sore forearm

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 9-year-old boy is brought to the emergency department in Ontario by his mother with pain in his left forearm. She says he fell off his bike yesterday. He lives with his mother and her partner. He has no medical history and takes no medications. He says little and looks at his mother before he answers.

He has abrasions on both knees and a bruise over the left shin. The left forearm is mildly tender with full movement, and an x-ray is normal. When he changes into a gown, the nurse sees several curved, loop-shaped bruises across his back and buttocks. He has not yet had a full examination.

- Temperature: 36.7°C oral
- Pulse: 96/minute
- Resp.: 20/minute
- BP: 104/64 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air
- Weight: 28 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following findings most strongly suggests that his injuries were inflicted?**

- A. Abrasions on both knees
- B. Bruise over the left shin
- C. Loop-shaped bruises on the back  **(key)**
- D. Normal forearm x-ray
- E. Tender left forearm

*Explanation:* Curved, loop-shaped bruises reproduce the shape of a folded cord or cable, and the back and buttocks are protected areas that a fall from a bike rarely strikes. A patterned bruise in a protected area points strongly to inflicted injury. Abrasions on both knees, a shin bruise and a sore forearm fit the bike fall his mother describes. A normal forearm x-ray rules out a fracture but says nothing about how he was hurt.

*Key feature:* Abuse (domestic) 2. In injured patients, notice injury patterns, delayed presentation, or repeat visits that hint at undisclosed abuse.

*Source:* Ward MG, Ornstein A, Niec A, Murray CL. Canadian Paediatric Society, Child and Youth Maltreatment Section. The medical assessment of bruising in suspected child maltreatment cases: a clinical perspective. Paediatr Child Health. 2013. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24426797/

#### Q2
**Which of the following is the most appropriate way to find all of his current injuries?**

- A. Ask him to point to sore areas
- B. Examine the injured limb only
- C. Forearm and wrist x-rays only
- D. Head-to-toe skin examination  **(key)**
- E. Skeletal survey

*Explanation:* The loop marks were found only when he undressed, which shows that injuries under clothing are missed unless the whole body is examined. This includes the scalp, ears, mouth, neck, back, buttocks and external genitalia, with each injury measured and documented. A guarded child who looks to his mother before he answers may not point to them. Examining or imaging only the forearm misses everything else. A skeletal survey is routine under 2 years of age. At 9 years, imaging is guided by pain, tenderness or deformity.

*Key feature:* Abuse (domestic) 3. When abuse is suspected, find all current and prior injuries using old charts, skeletal survey, or fundoscopy as needed.

*Source:* Ward MG, Ornstein A, Niec A, Murray CL. Canadian Paediatric Society, Child and Youth Maltreatment Section. The medical assessment of bruising in suspected child maltreatment cases: a clinical perspective. Paediatr Child Health. 2013. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24426797/

#### Q3
*Update:* Alone, he says his mother's partner hits him with a phone charger cord. His mother says a children's aid worker visited after a school report 3 months ago and the file is still open.

**Which of the following best describes your duty to report under Ontario law?**

- A. Ask the school to update the society
- B. Make a new report to the society now  **(key)**
- C. Report only if the worker asks
- D. Tell the mother to inform her worker
- E. No new report, since a file is open

*Explanation:* In Ontario every person with reasonable grounds to suspect that a child under 16 needs protection must report immediately and directly to a children's aid society, without relying on anyone else to report. The school's earlier report does not discharge his duty. The duty is also ongoing, so additional grounds need a further report even after earlier reports. His disclosure and the loop marks are new grounds, and an open file does not remove the duty. The report cannot be passed to the school or to his mother, and it does not wait for the worker to ask.

*Key feature:* Abuse (domestic) 6. Obtain the patient's consent to the plan, report to authorities when required, and secure safety for them and dependants.

*Source:* Ontario. Child, Youth and Family Services Act, 2017. S.O. 2017, c. 14, Sched. 1, section 125: duty to report a child in need of protection. 2017.

#### Q4
*Update:* A full examination and photographs are planned. He is frightened and refuses to take off his gown again.

**Which of the following team members is most appropriate to help him cope with the examination and photographs?**

- A. Child life specialist  **(key)**
- B. Hospital chaplain
- C. Pediatric psychiatrist
- D. Police victim services
- E. School guidance counsellor

*Explanation:* Child life specialists prepare children for stressful procedures with explanations and play suited to their age, and they teach coping strategies. Giving him some choice and control can make the examination and photographs possible without force. His fear is an expected reaction and not a psychiatric illness, so psychiatry has no role now. A chaplain offers spiritual care, not preparation for a procedure. Police victim services support victims through the justice process, and a school counsellor has no role in a hospital examination.

*Key feature:* Abuse (domestic) 5. Involve a team, including sexual assault services, abuse counsellors, and social workers, to intervene and support the patient.

*Source:* Romito B, Jewell J, Jackson M, American Academy of Pediatrics Committee on Hospital Care, Association of Child Life Professionals. Child life services. Pediatrics. 2021. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33372119/

### abuse-domestic-17: Infant who stopped breathing after a feed

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

You are working in a rural hospital with no CT scanner, 2 hours by road from a pediatric centre. A 6-month-old girl is brought to the emergency department by her mother's boyfriend. He says she went limp and stopped breathing for a few seconds after a feed an hour ago, then had a 2-minute generalized seizure. She has vomited three times since this morning. She has had no fever and no diarrhea. She lives with her mother, the boyfriend and her 18-month-old brother.

She is drowsy and irritable when handled. The anterior fontanelle is full. Her head circumference is above the 98th percentile, up from the 50th percentile at her 4-month visit. There are no bruises. Capillary glucose is 5.2 mmol/L.

- Temperature: 37.1°C rectal
- Pulse: 168/minute
- Resp.: 42/minute
- O2 sat: 97% on room air
- Weight: 7.4 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most likely cause of her presentation?**

- A. Bacterial meningitis
- B. Brief resolved unexplained event
- C. Infantile spasms
- D. Inflicted head injury  **(key)**
- E. Viral gastroenteritis

*Explanation:* A head circumference that has climbed from the 50th to above the 98th percentile in 2 months, with a full fontanelle, vomiting, apnea and a seizure, points to subdural collections from inflicted head injury. External bruising is often absent, and the history may describe no trauma at all. Meningitis can occur without fever in an infant, but it does not explain a head that has grown this fast over weeks. A brief resolved unexplained event requires a normal examination afterward. Infantile spasms are clusters of brief flexion or extension jerks, not a 2-minute generalized seizure. Gastroenteritis without diarrhea does not explain a full fontanelle.

*Key feature:* Abuse (domestic) 2. In injured patients, notice injury patterns, delayed presentation, or repeat visits that hint at undisclosed abuse.

*Source:* Shouldice M, Ward MGK, Nolan K, Cory E. Canadian Paediatric Society, Child and Youth Maltreatment Section. Medical assessment of suspected traumatic head injury due to child maltreatment (THI-CM). Paediatr Child Health. 2025. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40904407/

#### Q2
**Which of the following is the most appropriate plan for imaging her head?**

- A. Cranial ultrasound here before transfer
- B. MRI brain as an outpatient next week
- C. Observe here and image if seizures recur
- D. Skull x-rays here before transfer
- E. Urgent transfer for head CT  **(key)**

*Explanation:* She has a depressed level of consciousness, a full fontanelle and a seizure, so she needs head imaging today, and CT is the test of choice for acute, symptomatic head injury in a child. With no CT on site, the next step is urgent transfer with her airway and seizures managed on the way. Skull x-rays can show a fracture but miss intracranial blood. Cranial ultrasound through the fontanelle can miss thin subdural collections over the convexity and delays transfer. Observation or an outpatient MRI leaves a possible expanding hemorrhage untreated.

*Key feature:* Abuse (domestic) 3. When abuse is suspected, find all current and prior injuries using old charts, skeletal survey, or fundoscopy as needed.

*Source:* Shouldice M, Ward MGK, Nolan K, Cory E. Canadian Paediatric Society, Child and Youth Maltreatment Section. Medical assessment of suspected traumatic head injury due to child maltreatment (THI-CM). Paediatr Child Health. 2025. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40904407/

#### Q3
*Update:* At the pediatric centre, CT shows bilateral subdural hematomas of mixed density. There is no skull fracture. She is admitted to the pediatric ICU.

**Which of the following investigations are most appropriate to complete her assessment for abuse?**

Select 3.

- A. Abdominal ultrasound
- B. Coagulation studies  **(key)**
- C. Dilated fundoscopy  **(key)**
- D. Echocardiography
- E. Lumbar puncture
- F. Serum ammonia
- G. Skeletal survey  **(key)**
- H. Thyroid function tests

*Explanation:* Dilated fundoscopy by an experienced pediatric ophthalmologist is indicated for any infant with intracranial hemorrhage, ideally within 72 hours because retinal hemorrhages fade. In a systematic review, retinal hemorrhages were found in 78% of abusive head trauma and 5% of accidental head trauma. A skeletal survey is indicated under 2 years. Coagulation studies look for a bleeding disorder that could mimic abuse. Abdominal injury is screened with AST, ALT and lipase rather than ultrasound. Lumbar puncture adds risk and no answer once CT shows the cause. Ammonia, thyroid tests and echocardiography do not address the injuries in question.

*Key feature:* Abuse (domestic) 3. When abuse is suspected, find all current and prior injuries using old charts, skeletal survey, or fundoscopy as needed.

*Source:* Maguire SA, Watts PO, Shaw AD, and colleagues. Retinal haemorrhages and related findings in abusive and non-abusive head trauma: a systematic review. Eye (Lond). 2013. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23079748/

#### Q4
*Update:* The mother arrives and asks when her daughter can go home. Her 18-month-old son is at home with the boyfriend.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate medical assessment for her brother?**

- A. Ask the mother to watch him for bruises
- B. Examination and skeletal survey  **(key)**
- C. Examine the brother only if he has bruises
- D. Neuroimaging of the brother today
- E. Review the brother at his next well visit

*Explanation:* A household contact under 24 months of a seriously injured, abused child should have a physical examination and a skeletal survey whatever the examination shows. In a multicentre study, 12% of contacts under 24 months had an abusive fracture, and none of those fractures had signs on examination. Waiting for bruises or a routine visit leaves him in the same home with occult injuries undetected. Neuroimaging was used only for contacts under 6 months in that study and found no injuries, so it is not routine at 18 months. The finding also has to be reported to child welfare, who decide where both children can safely stay.

*Key feature:* Abuse (domestic) 6. Obtain the patient's consent to the plan, report to authorities when required, and secure safety for them and dependants.

*Source:* Lindberg DM, Shapiro RA, Laskey AL, and colleagues. Prevalence of abusive injuries in siblings and household contacts of physically abused children. Pediatrics. 2012. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22778300/

A 20-month-old boy is brought to the emergency department in Ontario by his mother at 23:00 with vomiting that began at 18:00. The last three vomits were green. Her partner cared for him this afternoon and told her that the boy tripped over a toy and fell onto his tummy on the carpet. He has had no fever or diarrhea. He walked at 12 months and has been well. He lives with his mother, her partner and her 5-month-old daughter.

He is pale and quiet. His upper abdomen is mildly distended, and he cries when it is pressed. There are no bruises on his abdomen or elsewhere. The chest is clear and the neurological examination is normal. AST is 64 U/L and ALT is 52 U/L.

- Temperature: 37.2°C rectal
- Pulse: 146/minute
- Resp.: 30/minute
- BP: 96/58 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air
- Weight: 11.5 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following features of his case most strongly suggests that he may have been abused?**

- A. Absence of bruises on his abdomen
- B. Arrival late in the evening
- C. Mechanism too minor for his signs  **(key)**
- D. Normal neurological examination
- E. Pale and quiet appearance

*Explanation:* Green vomiting with a distended, tender upper abdomen points to a serious injury inside the abdomen, such as a hematoma of the duodenum. A trip from standing onto carpet is far too minor to explain that. In a multicentre series of duodenal injuries in children aged 5 years or younger, abuse caused 20 of 32, including all 6 in children younger than 2 years. He is 20 months old. The absence of abdominal bruising does not make an injury accidental. He arrived 5 hours after the first vomit, which is not a delay in seeking care. His pallor and quietness show how unwell he is, not how he was hurt. A normal neurological examination points neither way.

*Key feature:* Abuse (domestic) 2. In injured patients, notice injury patterns, delayed presentation, or repeat visits that hint at undisclosed abuse.

*Source:* Sowrey L, Lawson KA, Garcia-Filion P, and colleagues. Duodenal injuries in the very young: child abuse? J Trauma Acute Care Surg. 2013. https://doi.org/10.1097/TA.0b013e3182788cb2

#### Q2
**Which of the following best describes what his AST and ALT results mean for his assessment?**

- A. Abdominal injury remains possible  **(key)**
- B. Abuse is an unlikely cause
- C. Liver and bowel injury are excluded
- D. Pancreatic injury is confirmed
- E. Viral gastroenteritis is likely

*Explanation:* Both enzymes are below the 80 U/L cutoff used to screen for abdominal injury in young children assessed for abuse. In a large multicentre study that cutoff had a sensitivity of only 77%, so about 1 in 4 children with an abdominal injury had lower values. His green vomiting, distension and upper abdominal tenderness are signs of injury in their own right. Enzymes below that cutoff therefore cannot exclude injury to the liver or bowel. Transaminases come from the liver and say nothing specific about the pancreas. Enzyme levels do not show how an injury happened. He has no fever or diarrhea, which argues against gastroenteritis.

*Key feature:* Abuse (domestic) 3. When abuse is suspected, find all current and prior injuries using old charts, skeletal survey, or fundoscopy as needed.

*Source:* Lindberg D, Makoroff K, Harper N, and colleagues, ULTRA Investigators. Utility of hepatic transaminases to recognize abuse in children. Pediatrics. 2009. https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2008-2348

#### Q3

- B. Abdominal x-ray series
- C. Admission for serial examinations
- D. CT abdomen with IV contrast  **(key)**
- E. Repeat AST and ALT in 6 hours

*Explanation:* For a child aged 24 months or younger with clinical findings suspicious for visceral injury from physical abuse, the American College of Radiology rates CT of the abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast as usually appropriate. He is 20 months old, with green vomiting, distension and upper abdominal tenderness. The same criteria rate abdominal ultrasound as usually not appropriate. *Key feature:* Abuse (domestic) 3. When abuse is suspected, find all current and prior injuries using old charts, skeletal survey, or fundoscopy as needed.
