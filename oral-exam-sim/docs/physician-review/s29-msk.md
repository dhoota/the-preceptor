### msk-16: Forearm pain after a scooter fall

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 31-year-old man presents to the emergency department 90 minutes after he fell from an electric scooter onto his outstretched right hand. He has pain and swelling in the right forearm and wrist. He is healthy, takes no medications and works as an electrician.

The distal half of the right forearm is swollen and angulated. The skin is intact. He holds the forearm in pronation and resists any attempt to rotate it. The elbow is not tender and flexes and extends fully. The radial pulse is strong, capillary refill is 2 seconds, and hand sensation and finger movement are normal.

- Pulse: 92/minute
- Resp.: 16/minute
- BP: 138/84 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air

#### Q1
*Update:* Forearm X-rays show a displaced and shortened fracture of the radial shaft at the junction of its middle and distal thirds. The ulnar shaft is intact.

**Which of the following associated injuries is most important to look for in this man now?**

- A. Distal radioulnar dislocation  **(key)**
- B. Perilunate dislocation
- C. Radial head dislocation
- D. Scaphoid waist fracture
- E. Triquetral dorsal chip fracture

*Explanation:* The radius and ulna are joined at both ends, so a displaced and shortened radial shaft fracture with an intact ulna has usually disrupted a joint as well. With the break at the junction of the middle and distal thirds, that joint is the distal radioulnar joint, the Galeazzi pattern. It is often missed because the eye stops at the obvious fracture. A radial head dislocation pairs with an ulnar fracture, and his elbow is not tender and moves fully. Scaphoid, triquetral and perilunate injuries follow a fall on the hand but do not explain a shortened radius.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 1. When one injury is found, look for its typical companions, such as calcaneal with lumbar fractures or neurovascular damage.

*Source:* Atesok KI, Jupiter JB, Weiss AP. Galeazzi fracture. J Am Acad Orthop Surg. 2011. https://doi.org/10.5435/00124635-201110000-00006

#### Q2
**Which of the following examination findings would best support this associated injury?**

- A. Anatomical snuffbox tenderness
- B. Loss of thumb interphalangeal flexion
- C. Numbness of the index finger pad
- D. Pain over the radial head
- E. Tender and prominent ulnar head  **(key)**

*Explanation:* A tender ulnar head that stands out at the wrist is the bedside sign of distal radioulnar joint disruption. Painful, blocked forearm rotation fits with it, and he already resists any rotation. Snuffbox tenderness points to a scaphoid fracture. Pain over the radial head would suggest an elbow injury, and his elbow is not tender. Loss of thumb interphalangeal flexion tests the anterior interosseous nerve and index pad numbness tests the median nerve. Nerve injuries are rare with this fracture and would not identify the joint injury.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 1. When one injury is found, look for its typical companions, such as calcaneal with lumbar fractures or neurovascular damage.

*Source:* Alajmi T. Galeazzi fracture dislocations: an illustrated review. Cureus. 2020. https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.9367

#### Q3
*Update:* The ulnar head is prominent on the back of the wrist and tender. Dedicated wrist and elbow X-rays are taken.

**Which of the following X-ray findings would suggest disruption of the distal radioulnar joint?**

Select 3.

- A. Fracture of the ulnar styloid base  **(key)**
- B. Posterior elbow fat pad
- C. Radial shortening of 2 mm
- D. Radial shortening of 7 mm  **(key)**
- E. Radiocapitellar line through the capitellum
- F. Scapholunate gap of 2 mm
- G. Volar tilt of 11 degrees
- H. Widened radioulnar gap on the AP view  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Plain film signs of distal radioulnar joint injury are a fracture at the base of the ulnar styloid, radial shortening of more than 5 mm relative to the ulna, and a widened gap between radius and ulna on the AP view. Subluxation of the ulna on a true lateral view is another sign. Shortening of 2 mm is below that threshold. A radiocapitellar line through the capitellum is normal and argues against a radial head dislocation. A scapholunate gap of 2 mm and a volar tilt of 11 degrees are normal wrist measurements. A posterior fat pad points to an elbow fracture, not a wrist joint injury.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 1. When one injury is found, look for its typical companions, such as calcaneal with lumbar fractures or neurovascular damage.

*Source:* Alajmi T. Galeazzi fracture dislocations: an illustrated review. Cureus. 2020. https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.9367

#### Q4
*Update:* The films show an ulnar styloid base fracture and radial shortening of 7 mm. His hand remains well perfused with normal nerve function.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate definitive plan for his forearm injury?**

- A. Closed reduction and long arm cast
- B. Functional forearm brace
- C. Open reduction and plate fixation  **(key)**
- D. Short arm cast and clinic review
- E. Sugar-tong splint for 6 weeks

*Explanation:* In an adult, a radial shaft fracture with distal radioulnar joint disruption needs open reduction and plate fixation of the radius, with the joint tested for stability at surgery. Muscle forces pull the distal radius short and rotate it, so closed care usually fails in adults. A closed reduction and long arm cast is the approach that works in children, not in a 31-year-old man. A functional brace and a short arm cast both leave the forearm free to rotate. A sugar-tong splint is a temporary splint while he awaits surgery, not definitive care.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 7. Consult surgery promptly for injuries needing urgent operative care like compartment syndrome or open fractures.

*Source:* Atesok KI, Jupiter JB, Weiss AP. Galeazzi fracture. J Am Acad Orthop Surg. 2011. https://doi.org/10.5435/00124635-201110000-00006

### msk-17: Foot pain after missing a step

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 41-year-old woman presents to the emergency department 2 hours after she missed the bottom step while carrying a laundry basket. Her right foot was pointed down when she landed on it and it twisted under her. She has severe pain in the middle of the foot and has not been able to put weight on it. She has hypothyroidism and takes levothyroxine.

The midfoot is swollen. There is bruising on the sole of the midfoot. She is tender over the base of the second metatarsal. Passive abduction of the forefoot causes severe pain. The navicular and the base of the fifth metatarsal are not tender. The dorsalis pedis pulse is palpable and sensation is normal.

- Temperature: 36.7°C oral
- Pulse: 92/minute
- Resp.: 16/minute
- BP: 128/76 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most likely injury in this woman?**

- A. Cuboid compression fracture
- B. Fifth metatarsal base fracture
- C. Navicular body fracture
- D. Plantar fascia rupture
- E. Tarsometatarsal joint injury  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Plantar bruising of the midfoot, tenderness at the base of the second metatarsal and pain on forefoot abduction after an axial load on a plantarflexed foot point to a Lisfranc tarsometatarsal injury. Plantar ecchymosis marks tearing of the plantar ligaments. A fifth metatarsal base fracture and a navicular fracture would cause tenderness at those sites, which she lacks. A cuboid compression fracture causes lateral midfoot pain. A plantar fascia rupture causes heel and arch pain, not tarsometatarsal tenderness.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 2. Let the injury pattern suggest its cause, like posterior shoulder dislocation after seizure or ulnar shaft fracture after assault.

*Source:* Ross G and colleagues. Plantar ecchymosis sign: a clinical aid to diagnosis of occult Lisfranc tarsometatarsal injuries. J Orthop Trauma. 1996. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8932671/

#### Q2
*Update:* Non-weight-bearing X-rays of the foot are reported as normal. She still cannot put any weight on the foot.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in her evaluation?**

- A. Ankle X-ray series
- B. CT of the midfoot  **(key)**
- C. Nuclear bone scan
- D. Oblique toe X-rays
- E. Plantar ultrasound

*Explanation:* Normal non-weight-bearing films do not exclude a Lisfranc injury, and missed injuries are common. Her plantar bruising and inability to bear weight justify CT, which shows small avulsions and subtle diastasis that plain films miss. Weight-bearing films are an option only if she can stand on the foot, and she cannot. Ankle films image the wrong region. A bone scan is slow and does not define alignment. Toe films and plantar ultrasound do not assess the tarsometatarsal joints.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 4. When a suspected fracture has normal first films, consider occult scaphoid, hip, or elbow injury and treat accordingly.

*Source:* Sherief TI and colleagues. Lisfranc injury: how frequently does it get missed? And how can we improve? Injury. 2007. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17214988/

#### Q3
**Which of the following structures is most at risk from this injury?**

- A. Common peroneal nerve
- B. Dorsalis pedis artery  **(key)**
- C. Posterior tibial artery
- D. Sural nerve behind the ankle
- E. Tibialis posterior tendon

*Explanation:* The dorsalis pedis artery dives into the sole between the bases of the first and second metatarsals, exactly where a Lisfranc injury displaces bone. A displaced injury can tear it or cause a compartment syndrome of the foot, so the pulse is rechecked after any swelling or manipulation. The common peroneal nerve is at risk at the fibular neck. The posterior tibial artery and tibialis posterior tendon pass behind the medial malleolus. The sural nerve runs along the lateral heel.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 1. When one injury is found, look for its typical companions, such as calcaneal with lumbar fractures or neurovascular damage.

*Source:* Walls RM, Hockberger RS, Gausche-Hill M, Erickson TB, Wilcox SR, editors. Rosen's Emergency Medicine: Concepts and Clinical Practice. 10th ed. Elsevier. 2023. Chapter 49, Ankle and Foot Injuries. https://www.us.elsevierhealth.com/rosens-emergency-medicine-concepts-and-clinical-practice-9780323757898.html

#### Q4
*Update:* CT shows 3 mm of widening between the bases of the first and second metatarsals, with a small bone flake at the base of the second metatarsal.

**Which of the following are the most appropriate parts of her emergency department plan?**

Select 3.

- A. Air cast walking boot
- B. Below knee posterior splint  **(key)**
- C. Crutches with no weight bearing  **(key)**
- D. Foot and ankle surgery referral  **(key)**
- E. Hard soled shoe
- F. Physiotherapy referral
- G. Repeat CT in 6 weeks
- H. Weight bearing as tolerated

*Explanation:* Diastasis of 3 mm between the first and second metatarsal bases with a fleck avulsion is an unstable Lisfranc injury. It needs a below knee splint, strict non-weight bearing on crutches and referral to a foot and ankle surgeon for fixation. A walking boot, a hard soled shoe or weight bearing as tolerated would suit a stable sprain and let the joint displace further. Physiotherapy comes after fixation. Repeat CT in 6 weeks would delay surgery past the window for anatomic reduction.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 7. Consult surgery promptly for injuries needing urgent operative care like compartment syndrome or open fractures.

*Source:* Ross G and colleagues. Plantar ecchymosis sign: a clinical aid to diagnosis of occult Lisfranc tarsometatarsal injuries. J Orthop Trauma. 1996. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8932671/

### msk-18: Older woman who tripped in her garden

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 71-year-old woman presents to the emergency department 90 minutes after she tripped over a garden hose and fell onto her outstretched left arm. She has severe left shoulder pain and cannot move the arm. This has never happened before. She has hypertension and osteoporosis. She takes ramipril and alendronate.

She holds the left arm slightly abducted and externally rotated. The rounded contour of the shoulder is lost and there is a fullness in front of the shoulder. The radial pulse is strong. She moves her fingers and her hand sensation is normal.

- Temperature: 36.6°C oral
- Pulse: 96/minute
- Resp.: 18/minute
- BP: 158/88 mmHg
- O2 sat: 97% on room air

#### Q1
**Which of the following examination findings should be documented to test the nerve most often injured with this dislocation?**

- A. Dorsal first web space sensation
- B. Elbow flexion strength
- C. Lateral deltoid sensation  **(key)**
- D. Lateral forearm sensation
- E. Wrist extension strength

*Explanation:* The axillary nerve winds around the surgical neck of the humerus and is the nerve most often injured in anterior dislocation. Sensation over the lateral deltoid is the practical bedside test, since deltoid strength cannot be tested in a dislocated shoulder. It is documented before and after reduction. Elbow flexion and lateral forearm sensation test the musculocutaneous nerve. Wrist extension and the dorsal first web space test the radial nerve. These nerves are injured less often.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 5. Document distal pulses and nerve function around every manipulation, and reduce prior to films when indicated.

*Source:* Robinson CM and colleagues. Injuries associated with traumatic anterior glenohumeral dislocations. J Bone Joint Surg Am. 2012. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22218378/

#### Q2
*Update:* Sensation over the lateral deltoid is reduced compared with the right side.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate next step before any attempt at reduction?**

- A. Closed reduction without imaging
- B. CT of the shoulder
- C. Scapular Y view after reduction only
- D. Shoulder X-rays before reduction  **(key)**
- E. Ultrasound of the rotator cuff

*Explanation:* The Fresno-Quebec rule, a derivation study that still awaits validation, calls for prereduction films in patients over 35 with a first episode or a blunt injury, and she has both. At her age a greater tuberosity or humeral neck fracture is common, and reducing a fracture-dislocation blind can displace it. Reduction without imaging suits a younger patient with a recurrent, atraumatic dislocation. CT is not needed before plain films. Films taken only after reduction find a fracture only after reduction may have displaced it. Ultrasound of the cuff comes later.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 5. Document distal pulses and nerve function around every manipulation, and reduce prior to films when indicated.

*Source:* Emond M and colleagues. Selective prereduction radiography in anterior shoulder dislocation: the Fresno-Quebec rule. J Emerg Med. 2018. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29861274/

#### Q3
*Update:* X-rays show an anterior dislocation with no fracture. After reduction, films show a concentric joint. Sensation over the lateral deltoid remains reduced.

**Which of the following associated injuries is most likely in this patient?**

- A. Acromioclavicular separation
- B. Anterior labral tear
- C. Axillary artery injury
- D. Long head of biceps rupture
- E. Rotator cuff tear  **(key)**

*Explanation:* A woman over 60 who dislocates in a low energy fall has a high rate of rotator cuff tear, and a cuff tear is more common again when an axillary nerve deficit is present. Both cause weakness of abduction after reduction, so each must be looked for when the other is found. An anterior labral tear is the typical lesion of young patients. Axillary artery injury is rare and her radial pulse is strong. Acromioclavicular separation and biceps rupture are not typical companions.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 1. When one injury is found, look for its typical companions, such as calcaneal with lumbar fractures or neurovascular damage.

*Source:* Robinson CM and colleagues. Injuries associated with traumatic anterior glenohumeral dislocations. J Bone Joint Surg Am. 2012. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22218378/

#### Q4
**Which of the following is the most appropriate follow-up for her after discharge in a sling?**

- A. Family physician review in 6 weeks
- B. MRI of the shoulder before discharge
- C. Nerve conduction studies in 1 week
- D. Orthopedic review within 2 weeks  **(key)**
- E. No follow-up unless pain persists

*Explanation:* An older patient with an axillary nerve deficit needs orthopedic review within about 2 weeks, with ultrasound or MRI of the cuff arranged there, because an acute cuff tear does best with early repair. Review at 6 weeks lets a repairable cuff tear retract. MRI before discharge is not needed in the emergency department. Nerve conduction studies are not informative until about 3 weeks after injury. Leaving follow-up to her pain misses a weak shoulder that may not hurt much.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 8. After diagnosis and initial care of an injury, arrange splinting, analgesia, and timely follow-up.

*Source:* Robinson CM and colleagues. Injuries associated with traumatic anterior glenohumeral dislocations. J Bone Joint Surg Am. 2012. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22218378/

### msk-19: Boy who fell off a trampoline

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 7-year-old boy is brought to the emergency department by his mother 1 hour after he fell off a backyard trampoline onto his outstretched left arm. He has pain in the forearm and elbow and will not move the arm. He is healthy. He was given intranasal fentanyl at triage and now rates his pain 4 out of 10.

The proximal forearm is swollen and angulated. The elbow is swollen and he resists any movement of it. The skin is intact. The radial pulse is present and capillary refill is 2 seconds.

- Temperature: 37.0°C oral
- Pulse: 118/minute
- Resp.: 22/minute
- BP: 104/64 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air
- Weight: 24 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most appropriate immobilization of his arm before X-rays?**

- A. Above elbow splint as it lies  **(key)**
- B. Below elbow volar splint
- C. Collar and cuff sling alone
- D. Removable wrist splint
- E. Sugar tong splint of the forearm

*Explanation:* He has a deformed proximal forearm and a swollen, painful elbow, so the arm is splinted as it lies in a long arm splint that holds the elbow and the wrist, the joints above and below the injury. Splinting in position limits pain and further injury, and the pulse and capillary refill are rechecked afterward. A below elbow volar splint and a removable wrist splint leave the elbow free. A sugar tong splint of the forearm limits rotation but lets the elbow flex. A collar and cuff sling alone does not hold the angulated fracture.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 8. After diagnosis and initial care of an injury, arrange splinting, analgesia, and timely follow-up.

*Source:* TREKK (Translating Emergency Knowledge for Kids). Bottom line recommendations: pediatric fractures. Version 4.0. 2025. https://cms.trekk.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/2025_04_22-Fractures-BLR-v4.0-Final.pdf

#### Q2
*Update:* Forearm X-rays show an angulated fracture of the proximal third of the ulna. The radius is intact.

**Which of the following associated injuries must be excluded on his X-rays?**

- A. Distal radioulnar joint dislocation
- B. Lateral condyle fracture
- C. Radial head dislocation  **(key)**
- D. Scaphoid waist fracture
- E. Supracondylar humerus fracture

*Explanation:* The radius and ulna form a ring, so an angulated ulnar fracture with an intact radius means the ring has also broken at a joint. At this level that is a radial head dislocation, the Monteggia lesion. On every lateral view a line along the radial shaft should pass through the capitellum. A distal radioulnar dislocation pairs with a distal radial shaft fracture, the Galeazzi pattern. Lateral condyle and supracondylar fractures do not explain an ulnar shaft fracture. Scaphoid fractures are rare at this age.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 1. When one injury is found, look for its typical companions, such as calcaneal with lumbar fractures or neurovascular damage.

*Source:* Ring D, Jupiter JB, Waters PM. Monteggia fractures in children and adults. J Am Acad Orthop Surg. 1998. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9682084/

#### Q3
*Update:* On the lateral elbow view, a line along the radial shaft passes in front of the capitellum. He cannot extend his thumb or fingers at the knuckles. He extends his wrist with radial deviation. Hand sensation is normal.

**Which of the following nerves is most likely injured in this boy?**

- A. Anterior interosseous nerve
- B. Posterior interosseous nerve  **(key)**
- C. Radial nerve in the spiral groove
- D. Superficial radial nerve
- E. Ulnar nerve at the elbow

*Explanation:* Loss of thumb and finger extension at the metacarpophalangeal joints, with wrist extension that deviates radially and normal sensation, is a posterior interosseous nerve palsy. The dislocated radial head stretches this nerve as it winds around the radial neck. A radial nerve injury in the spiral groove would also cause wrist drop and loss of dorsal hand sensation. - B. DIP and PIP splinted in extension
- C. Splint that holds only the DIP straight  **(key)**
- D. Ulnar gutter splint to the forearm
- E. Volar splint with the DIP in flexion

*Explanation:* A fingertip that droops, cannot be straightened actively and extends fully when pushed is a mallet finger from injury to the terminal extensor tendon, and his X-rays show no fracture. ### msk-37: Older woman who slipped in her kitchen

An 84-year-old woman is brought to the emergency department by ambulance 3 hours after she slipped on her wet kitchen floor and fell onto her right side. She remembers slipping and did not faint or hit her head. She has severe right shoulder pain and cannot lift the arm. She lives alone in an apartment and walks with a four-wheeled walker. She is right-handed. She has hypertension and takes amlodipine.

She holds the right arm against her body with her left hand. The right shoulder is swollen and tender, with bruising over the upper arm. She has no neck pain. The clavicle, elbow and chest wall are not tender. The skin is intact. The radial pulse is strong, and hand sensation and finger movement are normal.

- Temperature: 36.6°C oral
- Pulse: 90/minute
- Resp.: 18/minute
- BP: 152/84 mmHg
- O2 sat: 97% on room air
- Weight: 58 kg

#### Q1
*Update:* An AP X-ray of the right shoulder shows a fracture through the surgical neck of the humerus. The shaft is displaced medially by about half its width.

**Which of the following additional X-rays is most important for her now?**

- A. AP and lateral elbow views
- B. Axillary shoulder view  **(key)**
- C. Cervical spine series
- D. Dedicated clavicle views
- E. PA and lateral chest X-ray

*Explanation:* An AP film alone cannot show where the humeral head sits in relation to the glenoid, so a fracture with a dislocation can be missed. The shoulder trauma series adds a scapular Y view and an axillary view, and the axillary view is the one that confirms the head is in the joint. A fracture-dislocation changes the plan and must be found first. Her elbow, clavicle and chest wall are not tender, so films of them are unlikely to show an injury. She has no neck pain and did not hit her head, so cervical spine films are not needed.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 1. When one injury is found, look for its typical companions, such as calcaneal with lumbar fractures or neurovascular damage.

*Source:* Khmelnitskaya E and colleagues. Evaluation and management of proximal humerus fractures. Adv Orthop. 2012. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3535990/

#### Q2
*Update:* The axillary view shows the humeral head sitting in the glenoid. There is no other fracture.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate definitive plan for her fracture?**

- A. Closed reduction under sedation
- B. Coaptation splint and clinic review
- C. Open reduction and plate fixation
- D. Shoulder immobilizer for 6 weeks
- E. Sling and fracture clinic review  **(key)**

*Explanation:* In the PROFHER trial, adults with a displaced fracture involving the surgical neck did no better over 2 years with surgery than with a sling followed by rehabilitation. So a sling, analgesia and early fracture clinic review suit her, with gentle motion started as the clinic directs. Plate fixation adds anesthetic and surgical risk in an 84-year-old without a better shoulder. Closed manipulation under sedation was not part of the nonoperative care that matched surgery, and sedation carries its own risk at her age. A coaptation splint is used for humeral shaft fractures. Six weeks in an immobilizer delays the early motion that limits stiffness.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 8. After diagnosis and initial care of an injury, arrange splinting, analgesia, and timely follow-up.

*Source:* Rangan A and colleagues. Surgical vs nonsurgical treatment of adults with displaced fractures of the proximal humerus: the PROFHER randomized clinical trial. JAMA. 2015. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25756440/

#### Q3
*Update:* Her pain is controlled with acetaminophen and a small dose of hydromorphone. She wants to go home tonight.

**Which of the following is the most important assessment before she is discharged home?**

- A. Bone density measurement
- B. Mobility and self-care with one arm  **(key)**
- C. Right hand grip strength
- D. Serum calcium and vitamin D
- E. Shoulder range of motion

*Explanation:* She lives alone and walks with a four-wheeled walker, which needs two hands, and her dominant arm will be in a sling. Before she goes home, staff need to see her walk, transfer, use the toilet and manage simple tasks with one arm, with physiotherapy or occupational therapy where available. If she cannot, home supports or admission are arranged. In an emergency department cohort of older adults, an upper limb fracture or dislocation raised the odds of functional decline in the first week more than fivefold. Bone density and calcium belong to later bone health care, not to tonight's decision. Grip strength and shoulder motion do not show whether she can manage at home, and moving the shoulder hurts.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 8. After diagnosis and initial care of an injury, arrange splinting, analgesia, and timely follow-up.

*Source:* Wilber ST and colleagues. Short-term functional decline and service use in older emergency department patients with blunt injuries. Acad Emerg Med. 2010. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20653580/

#### Q4
**Which of the following is most important to ask her family physician to arrange after this fracture?**

- A. Fracture risk assessment  **(key)**
- B. Holter monitor for 48 hours
- C. MRI of the rotator cuff
- D. Nerve conduction studies
- E. Repeat shoulder X-ray in 1 year

*Explanation:* A fracture from a fall from standing height in an older adult is a fragility fracture, and a previous fracture raises the risk of the next one. The Osteoporosis Canada 2023 guideline suggests bone density testing for adults aged 50 or older after an osteoporosis-related fracture, with the 10 year fracture risk estimated using the Canadian FRAX tool and treatment started when the risk is high. The emergency visit is often the first chance to start this. She slipped and did not faint, so a Holter monitor has no indication. A rotator cuff MRI is not needed while the fracture heals. Nerve studies have no role with normal hand function. A routine X-ray in a year does nothing to lower her risk.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 8. After diagnosis and initial care of an injury, arrange splinting, analgesia, and timely follow-up.

*Source:* Morin SN and colleagues, Osteoporosis Canada. Clinical practice guideline for management of osteoporosis and fracture prevention in Canada: 2023 update. CMAJ. 2023. https://www.cmaj.ca/content/195/39/E1333

### msk-38: Buttock pain after a step down

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 78-year-old woman is brought to the emergency department by her son with 12 days of pain in her lower back and right buttock that spreads into the back of her right thigh. It began when she stepped down from a curb, and she can now walk only a few steps with a walker. Lying still eases it. She has polymyalgia rheumatica treated with prednisone 10 mg daily for 3 years. She takes no calcium, vitamin D or osteoporosis medication. Pressing over the right side of the sacrum reproduces her pain. Both hips move fully without pain. Straight leg raising to 70 degrees causes only buttock pain. Power, reflexes and sensation in both legs are normal. She is passing urine normally.

- Temperature: 36.8°C oral
- Pulse: 86/minute regular
- Resp.: 16/minute
- BP: 142/80 mmHg
- O2 sat: 96% on room air
- Weight: 52 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most likely source of the pain in the back of her right thigh?**

- A. Hamstring muscle tear
- B. Hip joint osteoarthritis
- C. L5 nerve root compression
- D. Piriformis syndrome
- E. Sacral insufficiency fracture  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Low back and buttock pain after little or no trauma, eased by lying still, with tenderness over the sacrum, fits a sacral insufficiency fracture. These fractures cause back, buttock and groin pain and occur mostly in older women with osteoporosis. Her thigh pain is referred rather than radicular, since straight leg raising produces only buttock pain and her power, reflexes and sensation are normal. Her hips move fully without pain, which argues against hip osteoarthritis. A hamstring tear and piriformis syndrome do not cause tenderness over the sacrum.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 3. Consider referred pain when examining a painful site, for instance hip pathology felt in the knee.

*Source:* Singh M, Balmaceno-Criss M, Knebel A, and colleagues. Sacroplasty for sacral insufficiency fractures: narrative literature review on patient selection, technical approaches, and outcomes. J Clin Med. 2024. https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm13041101

#### Q2
*Update:* X-rays of the pelvis and lumbar spine show diffuse osteopenia and no fracture. After analgesia she can still take only a few steps with her walker.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate plan for this patient now?**

- A. Admit for MRI and mobilization  **(key)**
- B. Bed rest at home for 6 weeks
- C. Discharge, as the X-rays are normal
- D. Epidural steroid injection
- E. Outpatient physiotherapy for sciatica

*Explanation:* Normal first X-rays do not exclude a sacral insufficiency fracture when the history and sacral tenderness point to one, so she is managed as having the fracture. MRI makes the diagnosis by showing high signal from bone edema on STIR sequences. First-line treatment is early mobilization, multimodal pain control and osteoporosis management, and a woman who can take only a few steps after analgesia needs that in hospital. Bed rest works against early mobilization. Discharge because the X-rays are normal misses the fracture. An epidural steroid injection and physiotherapy for sciatica treat nerve root pain, which she does not have.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 4. When a suspected fracture has normal first films, consider occult scaphoid, hip, or elbow injury and treat accordingly.

*Source:* Singh M, Balmaceno-Criss M, Knebel A, and colleagues. Sacroplasty for sacral insufficiency fractures: narrative literature review on patient selection, technical approaches, and outcomes. J Clin Med. 2024. https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm13041101

#### Q3
**Which of the following new findings in this patient would most require an urgent surgical consultation?**

- A. Bilateral fractures on imaging
- B. Pain on sitting upright
- C. Pain rated 8 out of 10
- D. Retention with saddle numbness  **(key)**
- E. Wrist fracture 2 years ago

*Explanation:* New urinary retention with numbness of the saddle area means the sacral nerve roots are failing. An emergency medicine review lists painless urinary retention and reduced perineal sensation among the red flags of cauda equina syndrome, confirms the diagnosis with MRI and treats it with surgical consultation for decompression. Severe pain, pain on sitting and fractures on both sides do not threaten the nerve roots, and sacral insufficiency fractures are managed first with mobilization, pain control and treatment of osteoporosis. A wrist fracture 2 years ago strengthens the case for osteoporosis treatment but is not urgent.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 7. Consult surgery promptly for injuries needing urgent operative care like compartment syndrome or open fractures.

*Source:* Long B, Koyfman A, Gottlieb M. Evaluation and management of cauda equina syndrome in the emergency department. Am J Emerg Med. 2020. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajem.2019.158402

#### Q4
**Which of the following underlying problems is most important to address before this patient goes home?**

- A. Hamstring weakness from inactivity
- B. Long-term glucocorticoid treatment  **(key)**
- C. Polymyalgia muscle inflammation
- D. Stepping down from a curb
- E. Use of a walker at home

*Explanation:* A pelvic fracture from a force no greater than stepping off a curb is a low-trauma fracture, and Osteoporosis Canada allows a clinical diagnosis of osteoporosis after a low-trauma pelvic fracture in anyone aged 50 years or older. Before treatment starts, the guideline advises assessing for secondary causes of osteoporosis, and it names inflammatory conditions treated with glucocorticoids among the conditions that can induce bone loss. She has taken prednisone for 3 years with no calcium, vitamin D or bone protection. The curb was the trigger, not the cause. Polymyalgia inflames muscles and joints rather than bone, and walker use and hamstring weakness do not explain a sacral fracture.

*Key feature:* Common fractures/MSK 2. Let the injury pattern suggest its cause, like posterior shoulder dislocation after seizure or ulnar shaft fracture after assault.

*Source:* Morin SN and colleagues, Osteoporosis Canada. Clinical practice guideline for management of osteoporosis and fracture prevention in Canada: 2023 update. CMAJ. 2023. https://www.cmaj.ca/content/195/39/E1333
