### first-trimester-bleeding-16: Cramping with an off-centre sac

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 32-year-old woman presents to the emergency department with 1 day of spotting and right-sided pelvic cramping. She is 7 weeks and 4 days by a certain last menstrual period. She has had two vaginal births. She was treated for chlamydia at age 20. She takes a prenatal vitamin.

Speculum examination shows a small amount of dark blood and a closed os. Bimanual examination finds a slightly enlarged uterus with tenderness at the right upper corner and no adnexal mass. Bedside ultrasound shows a gestational sac with a yolk sac in the right upper corner of the uterus. The myometrium between the sac and the serosa measures 3 mm. An echogenic line runs from the endometrial cavity to the sac.

- Temperature: 36.8°C oral
- Pulse: 92/minute
- Resp.: 16/minute
- BP: 118/74 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most likely interpretation of her bedside ultrasound findings?**

- A. Confirmed intrauterine pregnancy
- B. Eccentric normal intrauterine pregnancy
- C. Interstitial ectopic pregnancy  **(key)**
- D. Pseudogestational sac of a tubal ectopic
- E. Threatened miscarriage

*Explanation:* A sac at the edge of the uterus with only 3 mm of surrounding myometrium and an echogenic line joining it to the cavity fits an interstitial ectopic pregnancy. A yolk sac alone does not confirm an intrauterine pregnancy unless the sac sits within the cavity with a full myometrial mantle. A pseudosac has no yolk sac. Calling this a normal eccentric implantation or a threatened miscarriage would send home a pregnancy that can rupture with major bleeding.

*Key feature:* First trimester bleeding 4. Know that beta-hCG levels and ultrasound findings have limits when excluding ectopic pregnancy.

*Source:* Po L, Thomas J, Mills K, and colleagues. Guideline No. 414: management of pregnancy of unknown location and tubal and nontubal ectopic pregnancies. J Obstet Gynaecol Can. 2021. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33453378/

#### Q2
*Update:* Formal transvaginal ultrasound reports a 14 mm right interstitial gestational sac with a yolk sac and 3 mm of myometrium around it. There is no free fluid. Quantitative beta hCG is 8400 IU/L.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in her management?**

- A. Discharge with repeat hCG in 48 hours
- B. Early pregnancy clinic review in 1 week
- C. Methotrexate 50 mg/m2 IM from the ED
- D. Same day gynecology consultation  **(key)**
- E. Uterine aspiration in the emergency department

*Explanation:* A confirmed interstitial ectopic pregnancy needs a gynecologist the same day to choose between surgery and medical treatment. An hCG of 8400 IU/L is high for methotrexate, and that choice belongs to the treating gynecologist. Repeat hCG or a clinic visit in a week adds nothing to a diagnosis already made and leaves her at risk of rupture. Uterine aspiration cannot reach a sac embedded in the tubal part of the uterine wall.

*Key feature:* First trimester bleeding 2. Consider pregnancy in any vaginal bleeding, and if positive, work up to exclude ectopic pregnancy.

*Source:* Po L, Thomas J, Mills K, and colleagues. Guideline No. 414: management of pregnancy of unknown location and tubal and nontubal ectopic pregnancies. J Obstet Gynaecol Can. 2021. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33453378/

#### Q3
*Update:* While she waits, she has sudden severe right pelvic pain and feels faint. Her pulse is 128/minute and BP 92/58 mmHg. She is pale and sweaty.

**Which of the following are the most appropriate immediate actions for her now?**

Select 3.

- A. Crossmatch 4 units of red cells  **(key)**
- B. CT abdomen and pelvis with contrast
- C. Emergency gynecology for the operating room  **(key)**
- D. Methotrexate 50 mg/m2 IM
- E. Norepinephrine infusion
- F. Repeat formal ultrasound in radiology
- G. Repeat quantitative hCG
- H. Two large bore IV lines  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Sudden pain, pallor, a pulse of 128/minute and a BP of 92/58 mmHg mean the interstitial pregnancy has likely ruptured, and bleeding from this site can be brisk because the uterine wall is highly vascular. She needs large bore access, blood ready and an operating room. CT or a formal scan moves an unstable patient away from resuscitation. A repeat hCG and methotrexate do not stop bleeding. A vasopressor treats a number while the volume loss continues.

*Key feature:* First trimester bleeding 1. With acute vaginal bleeding, detect early shock using vital signs and orthostatic changes.

*Source:* Walls RM, Hockberger RS, Gausche-Hill M, Erickson TB, Wilcox SR, editors. Rosen's Emergency Medicine: Concepts and Clinical Practice. 10th ed. Elsevier. 2023. Chapter 173, Complications of Pregnancy. https://www.us.elsevierhealth.com/rosens-emergency-medicine-concepts-and-clinical-practice-9780323757898.html

### first-trimester-bleeding-17: Second visit for spotting

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 26-year-old woman returns to the emergency department for a planned recheck. She is 6 weeks and 2 days by last menstrual period and has had 3 days of spotting with mild left pelvic cramps. She had a left tubal ectopic pregnancy treated with methotrexate 3 years ago. She smokes 5 cigarettes a day. Two days ago her quantitative beta hCG was 1200 IU/L and transvaginal ultrasound showed no intrauterine or adnexal pregnancy and no free fluid. Today, on the same laboratory assay, it is 1460 IU/L.

Speculum examination shows a closed os with a small amount of blood. Bimanual examination finds mild left adnexal tenderness without a mass.

- Temperature: 36.6°C oral
- Pulse: 84/minute
- Resp.: 14/minute
- BP: 116/70 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most accurate interpretation of the change in her quantitative beta hCG?**

- A. Abnormal rise that confirms ectopic pregnancy
- B. Adequate rise for a viable intrauterine pregnancy
- C. Below the expected rise for a viable pregnancy  **(key)**
- D. Normal rise that makes ectopic unlikely
- E. Uninterpretable without a repeat ultrasound

*Explanation:* Her hCG rose from 1200 to 1460 IU/L, an increase of 260 IU/L or about 22% in 48 hours. For a starting value below 1500 IU/L, the minimum rise seen with a viable intrauterine pregnancy is about 49%. A slow rise occurs with both a failing intrauterine pregnancy and an ectopic pregnancy, so it does not confirm either. It is interpretable now and does not need a scan to give it meaning.

*Key feature:* First trimester bleeding 4. Know that beta-hCG levels and ultrasound findings have limits when excluding ectopic pregnancy.

*Source:* Barnhart KT, Guo W, Cary MS, and colleagues. Differences in serum human chorionic gonadotropin rise in early pregnancy by race and value at presentation. Obstet Gynecol. 2016. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27500326/

#### Q2
**Which of the following features in her history most raises the chance that this pregnancy is ectopic?**

- A. Cramping pain on the left side
- B. Previous tubal ectopic pregnancy  **(key)**
- C. Smoking 5 cigarettes a day
- D. Spotting for 3 days
- E. Two prior vaginal examinations

*Explanation:* A previous ectopic pregnancy is one of the strongest risk factors for another, whether the first was treated with methotrexate or surgery. Smoking raises the risk too, but to a lesser degree. One-sided cramping and spotting are common in both miscarriage and normal early pregnancy and do not locate the pregnancy. Prior vaginal examinations carry no risk.

*Key feature:* First trimester bleeding 2. Consider pregnancy in any vaginal bleeding, and if positive, work up to exclude ectopic pregnancy.

*Source:* American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists. Practice Bulletin No. 193: tubal ectopic pregnancy. Obstet Gynecol. 2018.

#### Q3
*Update:* Repeat transvaginal ultrasound today shows an empty uterus with a 7 mm endometrium, no adnexal mass and no free fluid. She remains comfortable with unchanged vital signs.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in her management?**

- A. CT abdomen and pelvis with contrast
- B. Methotrexate 50 mg/m2 IM from the ED
- C. Repeat hCG and ultrasound in 2 weeks
- D. Same day early pregnancy clinic review  **(key)**
- E. Serum progesterone to locate the pregnancy

*Explanation:* A stable pregnancy of unknown location with an abnormal hCG rise needs same day review by the early pregnancy or gynecology team. They may repeat the hCG, offer uterine aspiration or treat a presumed ectopic. Starting methotrexate from the ED without that plan risks ending a pregnancy whose location is unknown. A 2 week delay is unsafe with a prior ectopic. Progesterone does not locate a pregnancy, and CT adds radiation without answering the question.

*Key feature:* First trimester bleeding 4. Know that beta-hCG levels and ultrasound findings have limits when excluding ectopic pregnancy.

*Source:* Po L, Thomas J, Mills K, and colleagues. Guideline No. 414: management of pregnancy of unknown location and tubal and nontubal ectopic pregnancies. J Obstet Gynaecol Can. 2021. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33453378/

### first-trimester-bleeding-18: Bright red bleeding at thirteen weeks

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 30-year-old woman presents to the emergency department with 6 hours of light bright red vaginal bleeding after intercourse. She is 13 weeks and 2 days pregnant. This is her first pregnancy. A dating ultrasound at 8 weeks showed a single live intrauterine pregnancy. She has no pain. Her prenatal record states that her blood type is Rh D negative on serology and that RHD genotyping found weak D type 1. Her antibody screen was negative.

Bedside ultrasound shows a live intrauterine pregnancy with a heart rate of 158/minute and no subchorionic collection. The abdomen is soft and nontender.

- Temperature: 36.7°C oral
- Pulse: 80/minute
- Resp.: 14/minute
- BP: 112/68 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air

#### Q1
*Update:* Speculum examination shows a 1 cm smooth red mass protruding from a closed os. It bleeds when touched with a swab. There is a small amount of blood in the vault.

**Which of the following is the most likely source of her bleeding?**

- A. Cervical carcinoma
- B. Cervical ectropion
- C. Endocervical polyp  **(key)**
- D. Subchorionic hematoma
- E. Threatened miscarriage

*Explanation:* A smooth, red mass protruding from a closed os that bleeds on contact is an endocervical polyp, and it explains postcoital bleeding. Ectropion is a flat red area around the os, not a mass. Carcinoma looks irregular and friable rather than smooth. The ultrasound shows no subchorionic collection. A visible cervical source means this is not uterine bleeding from a threatened miscarriage.

*Key feature:* First trimester bleeding 3. Perform both a speculum and a bimanual pelvic exam in first trimester bleeding.

*Source:* Walls RM, Hockberger RS, Gausche-Hill M, Erickson TB, Wilcox SR, editors. Rosen's Emergency Medicine: Concepts and Clinical Practice. 10th ed. Elsevier. 2023. Chapter 30, Vaginal Bleeding. https://www.us.elsevierhealth.com/rosens-emergency-medicine-concepts-and-clinical-practice-9780323757898.html

#### Q2
**Using SOGC Guideline No. 448, which of the following is the most appropriate Rh immune globulin plan for her today?**

- A. Kleihauer Betke test before deciding
- B. Repeat RHD genotyping before deciding
- C. Rh immune globulin 120 mcg IM
- D. Rh immune globulin 300 mcg IM
- E. No Rh immune globulin  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Weak D types 1, 2 and 3 carry no risk of alloimmunization, so SOGC recommends no Rh immune globulin for these patients even after a bleed beyond 12 weeks. Her genotype is already known and does not need repeating. Had she been truly Rh D negative, or carried partial D or another weak D type, 300 mcg within 72 hours would have been suggested. A Kleihauer Betke test is not used to dose early pregnancy bleeding.

*Key feature:* First trimester bleeding 5. Check Rh status in pregnant patients with bleeding so anti-D prophylaxis can be offered.

*Source:* Fung-Kee-Fung K, Wong K, Walsh J, Hamel C, Clarke G. Guideline No. 448: prevention of Rh D alloimmunization. J Obstet Gynaecol Can. 2024. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38553007/

#### Q3
**Which of the following is the most appropriate advice for her at discharge?**

- A. Advise return for heavy bleeding or cramps  **(key)**
- B. Bed rest until the bleeding stops
- C. Polyp removal in the emergency department
- D. Vaginal progesterone until 20 weeks
- E. Weekly ultrasound until 20 weeks

*Explanation:* Bleeding from a polyp with a live pregnancy has a good outlook, and she should return if bleeding becomes heavy or cramping begins, since those suggest a uterine cause. Bed rest does not prevent pregnancy loss. Vaginal progesterone is considered for uterine bleeding in a patient with a prior miscarriage, and her bleeding is cervical with no prior loss. Removing a polyp in pregnancy is left to her obstetric provider, as it can bleed. Weekly scans do not change outcomes.

*Key feature:* First trimester bleeding 6. Before sending home a patient with a failed pregnancy, arrange counselling, emotional support, and follow-up care.

*Source:* National Institute for Health and Care Excellence. Ectopic pregnancy and miscarriage: diagnosis and initial management. NICE guideline NG126. 2019, updated 2026. https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng126

### first-trimester-bleeding-19: Spotting with a copper coil

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 30-year-old woman presents to the emergency department with 2 days of light vaginal bleeding and mild lower abdominal cramps. Her period is 2 weeks late, and a home pregnancy test this morning was positive. A copper IUD was inserted 18 months ago and she has felt its strings since. She has had one vaginal birth. She takes no medications and has no allergies.

She is comfortable and speaks in full sentences. Her abdomen is soft and not tender, with no guarding.

- Temperature: 36.7°C oral
- Pulse: 78/minute
- Resp.: 14/minute
- BP: 116/72 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air

#### Q1
**Which of the following diagnoses is most important to exclude first in this woman?**

- A. Ectopic pregnancy  **(key)**
- B. Expulsion of the IUD
- C. Molar pregnancy
- D. Pelvic inflammatory disease
- E. Uterine perforation by the IUD

*Explanation:* An IUD prevents intrauterine pregnancy far better than tubal pregnancy, so when a pregnancy does occur with an IUD in place, 15 to 50% are ectopic. Ectopic pregnancy is excluded first, even though she is comfortable with a soft abdomen, because rupture is the threat to her life. Expulsion or perforation of the IUD does not endanger her today. Molar pregnancy is uncommon and would be seen on the same ultrasound. Pelvic inflammatory disease is unlikely without fever, discharge or tenderness.

*Key feature:* First trimester bleeding 2. Consider pregnancy in any vaginal bleeding, and if positive, work up to exclude ectopic pregnancy.

*Source:* Black A, Guilbert E, Costescu D, and colleagues. Canadian contraception consensus (part 3 of 4): chapter 7. Intrauterine contraception. J Obstet Gynaecol Can. 2016. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jogc.2015.12.002

#### Q2
*Update:* Transvaginal ultrasound shows a single intrauterine gestational sac with a yolk sac and a fetal pole with cardiac activity. The IUD lies in the lower uterine cavity below the sac. The adnexa are normal. She wants to continue the pregnancy.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in her assessment?**

- A. Abdominal examination alone
- B. Repeat ultrasound in 1 week
- C. Serum hCG in 48 hours
- D. Speculum and bimanual examination  **(key)**
- E. No pelvic examination

*Explanation:* With an intrauterine pregnancy confirmed, a speculum examination looks for the IUD strings, the source of bleeding and the state of the os, and a bimanual examination checks uterine size and tenderness. Whether the strings can be seen decides how the device is managed. An abdominal examination alone cannot show the strings or the os. A live intrauterine pregnancy with normal adnexa makes a 48 hour hCG unnecessary. Rescanning in a week delays removal, which helps most when done early. Skipping the pelvic examination misses all of this.

*Key feature:* First trimester bleeding 3. Perform both a speculum and a bimanual pelvic exam in first trimester bleeding.

*Source:* Black A, Guilbert E, Costescu D, and colleagues. Canadian contraception consensus (part 3 of 4): chapter 7. Intrauterine contraception. J Obstet Gynaecol Can. 2016. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jogc.2015.12.002

#### Q3
*Update:* The IUD strings are visible at the external os. The os is closed with a small amount of blood in the vault. The uterus is nontender and 6 weeks in size.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate management of her IUD?**

- A. Leave it in until delivery
- B. Leave it until the next scan
- C. Remove it at 12 weeks
- D. Remove it by gentle traction now  **(key)**
- E. Remove it under general anesthesia

*Explanation:* With ectopic pregnancy excluded and the strings visible, the IUD is removed as soon as possible by gentle traction on the strings. Leaving it in raises the risk of miscarriage, of a septic miscarriage that can be life threatening, and of preterm birth. Early removal lowers these risks but not to the level of a pregnancy without an IUD, and she should be told so. Waiting for the next scan, for 12 weeks or until delivery keeps the risk in place. General anesthesia is not needed when the strings can be grasped.

*Key feature:* First trimester bleeding 3. Perform both a speculum and a bimanual pelvic exam in first trimester bleeding.

*Source:* Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. U.S. selected practice recommendations for contraceptive use, 2024. Intrauterine contraception. 2024. https://www.cdc.gov/contraception/hcp/usspr/intrauterine-contraception.html

### first-trimester-bleeding-20: Bleeding after two years without periods

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 24-year-old transgender man presents to the emergency department with 2 days of vaginal bleeding and right lower quadrant pain. He has taken testosterone for 2 years and has had no periods for 20 months. The triage note reads breakthrough bleeding on testosterone. He has a male partner and uses no contraception because he was told testosterone would prevent pregnancy. He has no other medical problems.

He has mild right lower quadrant tenderness without guarding. There is no rebound.

- Temperature: 36.9°C oral
- Pulse: 98/minute
- Resp.: 16/minute
- BP: 118/72 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most important initial test for him?**

- A. CBC and ferritin
- B. CT abdomen and pelvis
- C. Serum testosterone level
- D. Urinalysis and urine culture
- E. Urine pregnancy test  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Anyone with a uterus and ovaries who has vaginal bleeding and pelvic pain needs a pregnancy test, whatever the gender identity or hormone use. Testosterone does not reliably suppress ovulation, and new bleeding after long amenorrhea can be the first sign of pregnancy. A testosterone level does not answer the question. CT exposes a possible pregnancy to radiation, and a CBC or urinalysis cannot exclude an ectopic pregnancy.

*Key feature:* First trimester bleeding 2. Consider pregnancy in any vaginal bleeding, and if positive, work up to exclude ectopic pregnancy.

*Source:* Coleman E, Radix AE, Bouman WP, and colleagues. Standards of Care for the Health of Transgender and Gender Diverse People, Version 8. Int J Transgend Health. 2022.

#### Q2
*Update:* The urine pregnancy test is positive. He is distressed and says he dreads a pelvic examination.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate approach to his pelvic examination?**

- A. Bimanual examination only, to limit distress
- B. Defer both examinations to his family physician
- C. Examination only under procedural sedation
- D. Explain, seek consent and use a small speculum  **(key)**
- E. Replace the examination with bedside ultrasound

*Explanation:* He still needs a speculum and bimanual examination to judge the source and volume of bleeding, the state of the os and adnexal tenderness. Explaining each step, asking consent, letting him stop at any time and using a small, lubricated speculum respect his distress, as testosterone thins the vaginal lining. Ultrasound cannot see the cervix or the vault. Sedation is not needed for a cooperative patient, and deferral delays an ectopic workup.

*Key feature:* First trimester bleeding 3. Perform both a speculum and a bimanual pelvic exam in first trimester bleeding.

*Source:* Coleman E, Radix AE, Bouman WP, and colleagues. Standards of Care for the Health of Transgender and Gender Diverse People, Version 8. Int J Transgend Health. 2022.

#### Q3
*Update:* His os is closed with a small amount of blood. There is mild right adnexal tenderness. Quantitative beta hCG is 2900 IU/L. Bedside ultrasound shows no intrauterine pregnancy, no adnexal mass and no free fluid.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in his care?**

- A. Discharge, as the hCG is below 3500 IU/L
- B. Formal transvaginal ultrasound today  **(key)**
- C. Methotrexate 50 mg/m2 IM from the ED
- D. Repeat bedside ultrasound in 1 week
- E. Stop testosterone and repeat hCG in 1 week

*Explanation:* A symptomatic patient with an empty uterus on bedside ultrasound needs a formal transvaginal scan, which sees the adnexa far better, whatever the hCG level. No hCG value excludes an ectopic pregnancy, and ruptures occur below 3500 IU/L. Methotrexate is not given before the pregnancy is located. Stopping testosterone is right once pregnancy is confirmed but does not replace imaging. A week of delay risks rupture.

#### Q4
**Which of the following is the most accurate advice about testosterone and pregnancy for him?**

- A. Amenorrhea means pregnancy cannot occur
- B. Condoms are unneeded while on testosterone
- C. Testosterone can be continued in pregnancy
- D. Testosterone is reliable after 1 year of use
- E. Testosterone is unreliable as contraception  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Testosterone often stops menses but does not reliably prevent ovulation, so a transgender man with a sperm-producing partner needs contraception if he does not want to conceive. Amenorrhea on testosterone is not proof of infertility, as this pregnancy shows. Longer use does not make it reliable, so condoms or another method are still needed after a year or more. Testosterone can harm a developing fetus and should be stopped once pregnancy is confirmed.

*Key feature:* First trimester bleeding 2. Consider pregnancy in any vaginal bleeding, and if positive, work up to exclude ectopic pregnancy.

*Source:* Coleman E, Radix AE, Bouman WP, and colleagues. Standards of Care for the Health of Transgender and Gender Diverse People, Version 8. Int J Transgend Health. 2022.
