### ischemic-heart-disease-16: Chest pain brought in from home

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 59-year-old man is brought to the emergency department by ambulance with 50 minutes of heavy central chest pain and sweating. Paramedics recorded a 12-lead ECG at his home 30 minutes ago but did not transmit it, and brought him to your emergency department as usual. That ECG shows 3 mm ST elevation in V2 to V5 with ST depression in III and aVF. He chewed ASA 160 mg on scene. He has hypertension and type 2 diabetes. Two years ago he was admitted with a bleeding duodenal ulcer. He is pale and diaphoretic. Fine crackles are heard at both lung bases. There is no murmur and no peripheral edema.

- Pulse: 104/minute
- Resp.: 22/minute
- BP: 132/80 mmHg
- O2 sat: 95% on room air
- Weight: 88 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following system changes would most shorten the time to reperfusion for future patients like him?**

- A. Cardiology consultation on ED arrival
- B. Faster triage to a resuscitation bed
- C. Point-of-care troponin in the ambulance
- D. Prehospital catheterization lab activation  **(key)**
- E. Repeat ECG on emergency department arrival

*Explanation:* Transmitting a prehospital ECG and activating the catheterization laboratory from the field lets paramedics take a patient with STEMI straight to the laboratory, bypassing the emergency department. The CCS recommends this for regional STEMI systems because it shortens first medical contact to device time. Here a diagnostic ECG was recorded at his home 30 minutes ago and was not used. A cardiology consult, faster triage or a repeat ECG on arrival still leaves that delay in place. Troponin has no role in deciding reperfusion for STEMI.

*Key feature:* Ischemic heart disease 6. Arrange rapid reperfusion for STEMI using the best available option, PCI or thrombolysis.

*Source:* Wong GC, Welsford M, Ainsworth C, et al. 2019 Canadian Cardiovascular Society/Canadian Association of Interventional Cardiology guidelines on the acute management of ST-elevation myocardial infarction: focused update on regionalization and reperfusion. Can J Cardiol. 2019. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2018.11.031

#### Q2
*Update:* The catheterization laboratory is activated. He is given ticagrelor 180 mg and a heparin bolus before he leaves the department.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate addition to his dual antiplatelet therapy?**

- A. famotidine 20 mg PO twice daily
- B. misoprostol 200 mcg PO four times daily
- C. pantoprazole 40 mg PO daily  **(key)**
- D. sucralfate 1 g PO four times daily
- E. No gastric protection

*Explanation:* His bleeding duodenal ulcer 2 years ago places him at high risk of gastrointestinal bleeding on ASA with ticagrelor. The ESC recommends a proton pump inhibitor with dual antiplatelet therapy in patients at high risk of gastrointestinal bleeding, so pantoprazole is added. Famotidine, an H2 blocker, suppresses acid less completely than a proton pump inhibitor and is not the class the guideline names. Misoprostol mainly protects against NSAID injury and needs four daily doses that often cause diarrhea. Sucralfate coats an existing ulcer and has no proven role in preventing bleeding on antiplatelet drugs. Leaving him without gastric protection ignores a known ulcer bleed while he takes two antiplatelet drugs.

*Key feature:* Ischemic heart disease 7. Weigh risks and benefits of thrombolysis, PCI, pacing, or beta blockers before starting them in ACS.

*Source:* Byrne RA, Rossello X, Coughlan JJ, et al. 2023 ESC Guidelines for the management of acute coronary syndromes. Eur Heart J. 2023. https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehad191

#### Q3
**Which of the following is the most appropriate oxygen therapy for him at his current oxygen saturation?**

- A. Oxygen 6 L/minute by face mask
- B. Oxygen 2 L/minute by nasal prongs
- C. Oxygen 15 L/minute by nonrebreather
- D. Oxygen titrated to 100% saturation
- E. No supplemental oxygen  **(key)**

*Explanation:* His oxygen saturation is 95% on room air. DETO2X-AMI enrolled patients with suspected MI and a saturation of 90% or more, and routine oxygen did not reduce death at 1 year. Current guidelines therefore give oxygen only when saturation falls below 90%. Titrating oxygen to 100% saturation adds no benefit.

*Key feature:* Ischemic heart disease 7. Weigh risks and benefits of thrombolysis, PCI, pacing, or beta blockers before starting them in ACS.

*Source:* Hofmann R, James SK, Jernberg T, et al. Oxygen therapy in suspected acute myocardial infarction. N Engl J Med. 2017. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1706222

#### Q4
**Which of the following best describes his class based on the heart failure findings on examination?**

- A. CCS angina class II
- B. Killip class I
- C. Killip class II  **(key)**
- D. Killip class III
- E. Killip class IV

*Explanation:* Killip class II means rales in the lower lung fields, an S3 or raised venous pressure, without frank pulmonary edema. He has fine crackles at both bases with a BP of 132/80 mmHg, so he is class II. Class III is frank pulmonary edema and class IV is cardiogenic shock, and he has neither. Class I has no signs of heart failure. The CCS angina class grades exertional angina in stable disease and does not stage acute MI.

*Key feature:* Ischemic heart disease 3. Risk stratify confirmed or possible ACS, including atypical presentations, to guide management.

### ischemic-heart-disease-17: Jaw pressure far from a PCI centre

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

You are working in a community hospital emergency department without a catheterization laboratory. A 58-year-old woman is brought to the emergency department by ambulance with 50 minutes of central chest pressure radiating to her jaw. Paramedics reached her at 1310, and she arrived at 1330. Her ECG at 1334 shows 3 mm ST elevation in II, III and aVF with ST depression in I and aVL. The nearest PCI centre is 110 minutes away by land ambulance, and air transport is unavailable. Your team needs 30 minutes to get a patient out the door, and the receiving laboratory needs 20 minutes from arrival to device. She stopped her antihypertensive medications 2 weeks ago and takes no other medications. A CT coronary angiogram 4 years ago showed no coronary plaque. She has no history of bleeding, stroke or recent surgery. Her lungs are clear.

- Pulse: 84/minute
- Resp.: 18/minute
- BP: 188/114 mmHg
- O2 sat: 97% on room air
- Weight: 70 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following is her expected first medical contact to device time if she is transferred for PCI?**

- A. 140 minutes
- B. 160 minutes
- C. 180 minutes  **(key)**
- D. 200 minutes
- E. 210 minutes

*Explanation:* First medical contact was at 1310. Reaching your department took 20 minutes, getting her out the door takes 30, the drive takes 110 and the laboratory needs 20 more, so 20 + 30 + 110 + 20 = 180 minutes. The CCS guideline chooses primary PCI only when first medical contact to device can be kept within 120 minutes, so fibrinolysis is her reperfusion strategy. Leaving out either the arrival interval or the laboratory interval gives 160 minutes, which still exceeds 120 minutes.

*Key feature:* Ischemic heart disease 6. Arrange rapid reperfusion for STEMI using the best available option, PCI or thrombolysis.

*Source:* Wong GC, Welsford M, Ainsworth C, et al. 2019 Canadian Cardiovascular Society/Canadian Association of Interventional Cardiology guidelines on the acute management of ST-elevation myocardial infarction: focused update on regionalization and reperfusion. Can J Cardiol. 2019. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cjca.2018.11.031

#### Q2
**Which of the following is the most appropriate step before she receives tenecteplase?**

- A. Give half dose tenecteplase
- B. Give tenecteplase at once
- C. Lower BP below 180/110 mmHg  **(key)**
- D. Transfer for PCI instead
- E. No fibrinolysis for 24 hours

*Explanation:* Her BP of 188/114 mmHg is above the 180/110 mmHg threshold that the ESC lists as a relative contraindication to fibrinolysis, because severe hypertension raises the risk of intracranial hemorrhage. The pressure should be brought down promptly with an IV agent, then tenecteplase given without further delay. Half dose is for patients 75 or older, and at 58 she gets the full weight based dose. Transfer for PCI would take 180 minutes, and withholding fibrinolysis for a day forfeits its benefit.

*Key feature:* Ischemic heart disease 7. Weigh risks and benefits of thrombolysis, PCI, pacing, or beta blockers before starting them in ACS.

*Source:* Byrne RA, Rossello X, Coughlan JJ, et al. 2023 ESC Guidelines for the management of acute coronary syndromes. Eur Heart J. 2023. https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehad191

#### Q3
**Which of the following statements about her CT coronary angiogram 4 years ago is most accurate?**

- A. It does not exclude a new occlusion  **(key)**
- B. It favours a false positive ECG
- C. It makes pericarditis more likely
- D. It makes vasospasm the likely cause
- E. It rules out coronary thrombosis

*Explanation:* Her current ECG shows territorial ST elevation with reciprocal depression and ongoing pressure, which is an acute occlusion until proven otherwise. A CT coronary angiogram describes the arteries only at the time it was done, and plaque can develop over 4 years, particularly with hypertension. The old scan does not make the ECG a false positive or point to pericarditis, which causes diffuse rather than inferior ST elevation. It does not make vasospasm the likely cause or rule out thrombosis.

*Key feature:* Ischemic heart disease 4. Do not exclude ACS on one negative test or weak clues like antacid relief, chest wall tenderness, or pain resolution.

*Source:* Gulati M, Levy PD, Mukherjee D, et al. 2021 AHA/ACC/ASE/CHEST/SAEM/SCCT/SCMR guideline for the evaluation and diagnosis of chest pain. J Am Coll Cardiol. 2021. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2021.07.053

#### Q4
**Which of the following best explains the ST depression in leads I and aVL on her ECG?**

- A. Digoxin effect on repolarization
- B. Lateral subendocardial ischemia
- C. Left ventricular hypertrophy strain
- D. Rate-related repolarization change
- E. Reciprocal change of inferior injury  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Leads I and aVL face the opposite side of the heart from III and aVF, so ST depression there mirrors the inferior ST elevation. Reciprocal depression makes an acute inferior occlusion far more likely than a mimic. She takes no digoxin, her pulse is 84/minute so the change is not rate related, and hypertrophy strain affects the lateral leads with tall voltages rather than mirroring inferior elevation. Primary lateral subendocardial ischemia does not explain the inferior ST elevation.

*Key feature:* Ischemic heart disease 2. Interpret ECGs accurately in possible ACS, catching subtle ischemia and mimics like pericarditis.

*Source:* Byrne RA, Rossello X, Coughlan JJ, et al. 2023 ESC Guidelines for the management of acute coronary syndromes. Eur Heart J. 2023. https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehad191

### ischemic-heart-disease-18: Breathless with a pacemaker

A dual chamber pacemaker was placed 4 years ago for complete heart block. He has type 2 diabetes and hypertension, and takes metformin and amlodipine. He is pale and clammy. There are crackles at both lung bases and no peripheral edema. His ECG shows a ventricular paced rhythm at 80/minute. In V2 there is 5 mm of ST elevation after an S wave that is 16 mm deep. No lead shows concordant ST elevation or concordant ST depression.

- Temperature: 36.9°C oral
- Pulse: 80/minute
- Resp.: 26/minute
- BP: 108/66 mmHg
- O2 sat: 91% on room air
- Weight: 76 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most likely cause of his breathlessness at this point?**

- A. Acute coronary syndrome  **(key)**
- B. Community-acquired pneumonia
- C. COPD exacerbation
- D. Hypertensive pulmonary edema
- E. Pacemaker lead failure

*Explanation:* Breathlessness of 90 minutes with nausea, sweating, pallor and basal crackles in an 81-year-old with diabetes is an acute coronary syndrome until proven otherwise. Older adults and people with diabetes often present with dyspnea rather than chest pain, and his denial of pain does not lower the likelihood. He has no cough or fever, which argues against pneumonia. COPD is not among his conditions. His BP of 108/66 mmHg does not fit hypertensive pulmonary edema. The ECG shows a paced rhythm at 80/minute, so the pacemaker is capturing and lead failure does not explain his symptoms.

*Key feature:* Ischemic heart disease 5. Consider ACS in atypical presentations such as fatigue or dyspnea, particularly in elderly, diabetic, female, or cocaine using patients.

*Source:* Gulati M, Levy PD, Mukherjee D, et al. 2021 AHA/ACC/ASE/CHEST/SAEM/SCCT/SCMR guideline for the evaluation and diagnosis of chest pain. J Am Coll Cardiol. 2021. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2021.07.053

#### Q2
**Which of the following best describes the ST segment finding in lead V2 of his paced ECG?**

- A. Concordant ST elevation
- B. Excessively discordant ST elevation  **(key)**
- C. Expected paced repolarization change
- D. Left ventricular aneurysm pattern
- E. Loss of ventricular capture

*Explanation:* In a paced rhythm the ST segment normally moves opposite to the main QRS deflection. The modified Sgarbossa criteria call discordant ST elevation abnormal when it is 25% or more of the depth of the preceding S wave. Here 5 mm divided by 16 mm is about 0.31, which meets the criterion and suggests acute coronary occlusion. The elevation is not concordant, because it follows a negative S wave. A paced rhythm at 80/minute shows that capture is intact.

*Key feature:* Ischemic heart disease 2. Interpret ECGs accurately in possible ACS, catching subtle ischemia and mimics like pericarditis.

*Source:* Dodd KW, Zvosec DL, Hart MA, et al. Electrocardiographic diagnosis of acute coronary occlusion myocardial infarction in ventricular paced rhythm using the modified Sgarbossa criteria. Ann Emerg Med. 2021. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2021.03.036

#### Q3

- A. CT pulmonary angiography
- B. Pacemaker interrogation first
- C. Repeat troponin in 2 hours
- D. Tenecteplase in the department
- E. Urgent coronary angiography  **(key)**

*Explanation:* A positive modified Sgarbossa criterion in a paced rhythm, with ongoing ischemic symptoms, is managed as a STEMI equivalent. His diaphoresis, nausea and crackles fit ischemia with heart failure better than pulmonary embolism.

*Key feature:* Ischemic heart disease 6. Arrange rapid reperfusion for STEMI using the best available option, PCI or thrombolysis.

*Source:* Byrne RA, Rossello X, Coughlan JJ, et al. 2023 ESC Guidelines for the management of acute coronary syndromes. Eur Heart J. 2023. https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehad191
