### delirium-agitation-16: Sleepy and not herself

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

An 86-year-old woman is brought to the emergency department by her son because she has been sleepy and not herself for 3 days. She sits quietly, answers slowly and dozes off in the middle of sentences. Her son says she lives alone, manages her own pills and banking, and finished the newspaper crossword every day until last week. Eight days ago her family physician started pregabalin 150 mg twice daily for postherpetic neuralgia. She also takes amlodipine and has chronic kidney disease with a baseline eGFR of 24 mL/minute. She is oriented to place but not to the date. She cannot recite the months of the year backward past October. She recalls one of three words at 5 minutes. There is no focal neurological deficit. Capillary glucose is 6.1 mmol/L.

- Temperature: 36.8°C oral
- Pulse: 72/minute
- Resp.: 14/minute
- BP: 138/76 mmHg
- O2 sat: 96% on room air

#### Q1
**Which of the following findings best distinguishes delirium from dementia in this patient?**

- A. Age of 86 years with kidney disease
- B. Decline from an intact baseline in days  **(key)**
- C. Disorientation to the current date
- D. Poor recall of three words at 5 minutes
- E. Slow speech with long pauses

*Explanation:* Her son describes a woman who ran her own affairs and did the crossword until last week, and who changed over 3 days. An acute change from a known baseline, together with inattention on the months backward, is what defines delirium. Disorientation to the date and poor word recall occur in both delirium and dementia, so they cannot separate them. Advanced age and kidney disease are risk factors, not distinguishing features. Slow speech is also seen with depression and with dementia.

*Key feature:* Delirium/agitation 1. In an agitated patient, look for features that separate delirium from dementia and from psychosis.

*Source:* Canadian Coalition for Seniors' Mental Health. National guidelines for seniors' mental health. The assessment and treatment of delirium. 2006, updated 2014. https://ccsmh.ca/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/2014-ccsmh-Guideline-Update-Delirium.pdf

#### Q2
*Update:* Sodium is 138 mmol/L, calcium 2.34 mmol/L and creatinine 168 umol/L, unchanged from her baseline. The CBC, urinalysis and chest radiograph are normal.

**Which of the following is the most likely cause of her current presentation?**

- A. Hyponatremia from reduced oral intake
- B. Major depression with psychomotor slowing
- C. Occult urinary tract infection
- D. Pregabalin accumulation in kidney disease  **(key)**
- E. Progression of an undiagnosed dementia

*Explanation:* Pregabalin is cleared almost entirely by the kidneys, and with an eGFR of 24 mL/minute her dose of 300 mg a day is about double the usual maximum of 150 mg a day for this level of function. Sedation and confusion that began within days of starting it fit accumulation. The 2023 Beers Criteria advise reducing the dose of gabapentinoids when kidney function is reduced. Her sodium and urinalysis are normal. Depression and dementia do not produce a change over 3 days with drowsiness and inattention.

*Key feature:* Delirium/agitation 2. Keep a wide differential for delirium, including structural, toxic, withdrawal, and metabolic causes, and quickly treat reversible ones.

*Source:* American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria Update Expert Panel. American Geriatrics Society 2023 updated AGS Beers Criteria for potentially inappropriate medication use in older adults. Journal of the American Geriatrics Society 2023. https://doi.org/10.1111/jgs.18372

#### Q3
**Which of the following is the most appropriate management of her delirium now?**

- A. Change pregabalin to gabapentin 300 mg TID
- B. haloperidol 0.5 mg PO twice daily
- C. Hold pregabalin and treat pain otherwise  **(key)**
- D. quetiapine 25 mg PO at bedtime
- E. Start donepezil 5 mg PO daily

*Explanation:* Treating delirium means removing its cause, so the pregabalin is held and her neuralgia is managed with measures that do not depend on kidney clearance, such as a topical agent. Gabapentin is also renally cleared and sedating, so switching to it repeats the problem. Her delirium is hypoactive and she is not distressed or unsafe, so an antipsychotic such as haloperidol or quetiapine adds sedation without benefit. *Key feature:* Delirium/agitation 2. Keep a wide differential for delirium, including structural, toxic, withdrawal, and metabolic causes, and quickly treat reversible ones.

*Source:* Canadian Coalition for Seniors' Mental Health. National guidelines for seniors' mental health. The assessment and treatment of delirium. 2006, updated 2014. https://ccsmh.ca/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/2014-ccsmh-Guideline-Update-Delirium.pdf

#### Q4
*Update:* She says she wants to go home tonight. When asked, she cannot say what could happen if she is alone at home while this drowsy, and repeats that she is fine.

**Which of the following best describes her capacity to make this discharge decision?**

- A. Capable, as she expresses a clear choice
- B. Capable, as she knows she is in hospital
- C. Incapable, as delirium removes capacity
- D. Incapable, as she cannot weigh the risks  **(key)**
- E. Undetermined until psychiatry assesses her

*Explanation:* Capacity is specific to the decision and needs four abilities: to express a choice, to understand the information, to appreciate how it applies to her, and to reason about the options. She states a choice, but she cannot say what could happen if she goes home in this state, so she fails appreciation and reasoning. Knowing where she is shows orientation, not capacity. *Key feature:* Delirium/agitation 5. Assess decision making capacity in delirium and decide whether a surrogate decision maker or involuntary treatment is needed.

*Source:* Appelbaum PS. Assessment of patients' competence to consent to treatment. New England Journal of Medicine 2007. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMcp074045

### delirium-agitation-17: Drooling and drowsy at the group home

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 38-year-old woman is brought to the emergency department by staff from her group home for 2 days of confusion. She has schizophrenia, stable for 6 years on clozapine 400 mg at bedtime. She smoked 25 cigarettes a day until she moved into the smoke-free home 12 days ago. Staff say she has been drooling, fell asleep twice at meals and yesterday could not find her own room. When stressed she sometimes hears voices, and she did so last night. She is drowsy but rousable, drifts off during questions and cannot spell WORLD backward. There are brief jerks of both arms. Muscle tone is normal. Capillary glucose is 5.4 mmol/L.

- Temperature: 37.1°C oral
- Pulse: 118/minute
- Resp.: 16/minute
- BP: 96/58 mmHg
- O2 sat: 97% on room air

#### Q1
**Which of the following findings best indicates delirium rather than a relapse of her schizophrenia?**

- A. Auditory hallucinations when stressed
- B. Drowsiness with impaired attention  **(key)**
- C. Flat affect with little speech
- D. Paranoid ideas about her caregivers
- E. Withdrawal from group activities

*Explanation:* A reduced level of arousal and inattention, shown by drowsiness, drifting off during questions and failing WORLD backward, are core features of delirium and are not typical of a psychotic relapse. Voices under stress are part of her usual illness. Flat affect, paranoid ideas and social withdrawal are positive or negative symptoms of schizophrenia and occur with a clear sensorium. Labelling her confusion as relapse risks missing a medical cause.

*Key feature:* Delirium/agitation 1. In an agitated patient, look for features that separate delirium from dementia and from psychosis.

*Source:* Canadian Coalition for Seniors' Mental Health. National guidelines for seniors' mental health. The assessment and treatment of delirium. 2006, updated 2014. https://ccsmh.ca/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/2014-ccsmh-Guideline-Update-Delirium.pdf

#### Q2
*Update:* Neutrophils are 5.8 x 10^9/L. Sodium is 139 mmol/L. Troponin and creatine kinase are normal.

**Which of the following is the most likely cause of her presentation?**

- A. Agranulocytosis with sepsis from clozapine
- B. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome
- C. Nicotine withdrawal delirium
- D. Rising clozapine level after smoking stopped  **(key)**
- E. Water intoxication with hyponatremia

*Explanation:* Chemicals in tobacco smoke induce CYP1A2, the main enzyme that clears clozapine. When a heavy smoker stops, induction wanes over about 1 to 2 weeks and clozapine levels can rise by half or more on the same dose. Sedation, drooling, tachycardia, low blood pressure, myoclonus and delirium 12 days after she stopped fit toxicity. Her neutrophils are normal, her sodium is normal, and she has no fever, rigidity or raised creatine kinase to suggest neuroleptic malignant syndrome. Nicotine withdrawal causes irritability and craving, not myoclonus and drooling.

*Key feature:* Delirium/agitation 2. Keep a wide differential for delirium, including structural, toxic, withdrawal, and metabolic causes, and quickly treat reversible ones.

*Source:* Taylor DM, Barnes TRE, Young AH. The Maudsley Prescribing Guidelines in Psychiatry. 14th edition. Wiley Blackwell. 2021.

#### Q3
**Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in managing her clozapine?**

- A. Add glycopyrrolate for the hypersalivation
- B. Continue clozapine with nicotine patches
- C. Hold clozapine and send a drug level  **(key)**
- D. Restart clozapine at 400 mg in 3 days
- E. Switch to olanzapine 10 mg PO nightly

*Explanation:* The drug is held while she is toxic and a clozapine level confirms the cause and guides the new dose. After smoking stops, the dose is usually cut by about a quarter at first, with further cuts guided by repeat levels. Nicotine patches do not induce CYP1A2, because the inducers are the hydrocarbons in smoke, so continuing the same dose leaves the level high. Restarting at 400 mg repeats the toxicity, and after more than 48 hours off clozapine it must be titrated up again from a low dose. Glycopyrrolate treats one symptom only. Switching antipsychotics is a psychiatric decision for later.

*Key feature:* Delirium/agitation 2. Keep a wide differential for delirium, including structural, toxic, withdrawal, and metabolic causes, and quickly treat reversible ones.

*Source:* Taylor DM, Barnes TRE, Young AH. The Maudsley Prescribing Guidelines in Psychiatry. 14th edition. Wiley Blackwell. 2021.

#### Q4
**Which of the following is the most important safety measure while she remains in the department?**

- A. Four point restraints to prevent falls
- B. Hallway bed near the nursing station
- C. Seclusion room with the door locked
- D. Security guard posted at the doorway
- E. Sitter with seizure precautions  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Clozapine lowers the seizure threshold in a dose related way, and her myoclonus, drowsiness and drooling put her at risk of a seizure, a fall and aspiration. Constant observation with suction and padded rails lets staff act at once. Restraints add injury and aspiration risk in a drowsy patient and are not needed when she is not violent. A hallway bed gives only intermittent observation. Seclusion and a security guard address violence, which she has not shown, and leave her unobserved medically.

*Key feature:* Delirium/agitation 3. Protect everyone's safety, including staff, families, other patients, and the patient, when managing agitation.

*Source:* Taylor DM, Barnes TRE, Young AH. The Maudsley Prescribing Guidelines in Psychiatry. 14th edition. Wiley Blackwell. 2021.

Lorazepam and haloperidol given alone have a slower onset than this combination. Rapid control matters here because he is fighting four officers and cannot yet be examined.

*Key feature:* Delirium/agitation 4. If restraint is needed, sedate with medication first, then monitor closely for as long as restraints remain.

*Source:* Taylor DM, Yap CYL, Knott JC, et al. Midazolam-droperidol, droperidol, or olanzapine for acute agitation. A randomized clinical trial. Annals of Emergency Medicine 2017. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2016.07.033

#### Q2
*Update:* Six minutes after the combination he is asleep and snoring softly, and the restraints are being removed.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate monitoring for him now?**

- A. Blood glucose every hour
- B. Continuous oximetry and end-tidal CO2  **(key)**
- C. Glasgow Coma Scale every 4 hours
- D. Observation from the nursing station
- E. Vital signs when the nurse is free

*Explanation:* A benzodiazepine with an antipsychotic can cause airway obstruction and hypoventilation, and snoring is an early warning sign, so he needs continuous oximetry with end-tidal CO2 and a nurse at the bedside until he is rousable. Observation from a distance or vital signs when convenient can miss hypoventilation. Hourly glucose checks are not needed with a normal glucose. A GCS every 4 hours is far too infrequent after sedation.

*Key feature:* Delirium/agitation 4. If restraint is needed, sedate with medication first, then monitor closely for as long as restraints remain.

*Source:* Knox DK, Holloman GH Jr. Use and avoidance of seclusion and restraint. Consensus statement of the American Association for Emergency Psychiatry Project BETA Seclusion and Restraint Workgroup. Western Journal of Emergency Medicine 2012. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22461919/

#### Q3
**Which of the following is the most important next step now that he is sedated?**

- A. Examine fully for a medical cause  **(key)**
- B. Leave him to sleep off the drugs
- C. Refer directly to psychiatry
- D. Remove monitoring once asleep
- E. Transfer him to police custody

*Explanation:* Sedation is the first chance to examine him, and the Project BETA consensus stresses finding and treating the underlying cause of agitation rather than only medicating it. A full examination, core temperature and tests such as electrolytes, creatine kinase and a toxicological assessment look for head injury, toxidromes, hyperthermia or infection. Assuming intoxication, referring to psychiatry or handing him to police can miss a treatable medical cause. Monitoring must continue while he is sedated.

*Key feature:* Delirium/agitation 2. Keep a wide differential for delirium, including structural, toxic, withdrawal, and metabolic causes, and quickly treat reversible ones.

*Source:* Wilson MP, Pepper D, Currier GW, et al. The psychopharmacology of agitation. Consensus statement of the American Association for Emergency Psychiatry Project BETA Psychopharmacology Workgroup. Western Journal of Emergency Medicine 2012. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22461918/

### delirium-agitation-38: Demanding to leave at 04:00

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 66-year-old man with COPD has been boarding in the emergency department for 26 hours awaiting a medical bed for an exacerbation that is now improving. He smokes 30 cigarettes a day. At 04:00 he is pacing, irritable and demanding to leave so that he can smoke. He has shouted at his nurse but has not threatened anyone. He is alert, knows the date and place and follows a detailed conversation. He explains that leaving may worsen his breathing and could lead to intubation, and says he accepts that risk because he cannot stand being kept in. He has no history of cognitive impairment. He drinks no alcohol and takes no sedatives. A venous blood gas at 02:00 showed a pH of 7.38 and a PCO2 of 48 mmHg.

- Temperature: 36.9°C oral
- Pulse: 98/minute
- Resp.: 20/minute
- BP: 148/86 mmHg
- O2 sat: 92% on 1 L/minute by nasal prongs

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most likely cause of his agitation at 04:00?**

- A. Alcohol withdrawal
- B. Benzodiazepine withdrawal
- C. Hypercapnic encephalopathy
- D. Hypoxic delirium
- E. Nicotine withdrawal  **(key)**

*Explanation:* He smokes 30 cigarettes a day and has gone 26 hours without one, and he is restless, irritable and focused on smoking. Anger, impatience and restlessness are recognized symptoms of tobacco abstinence. He drinks no alcohol and takes no sedatives, so alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal have no basis. A pH of 7.38 with a PCO2 of 48 mmHg and his alert, oriented state argue against hypercapnic encephalopathy. A saturation of 92% on 1 L/minute is at his target, and he follows a detailed conversation, so hypoxic delirium is unlikely.

*Key feature:* Delirium/agitation 2. Keep a wide differential for delirium, including structural, toxic, withdrawal, and metabolic causes, and quickly treat reversible ones.

*Source:* Hughes JR. Effects of abstinence from tobacco. Valid symptoms and time course. Nicotine and Tobacco Research 2007. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17365764/

#### Q2
**Which of the following best shows that he has capacity to refuse ongoing admission?**

- A. He has no history of dementia
- B. He is alert and walking steadily
- C. He speaks in full sentences
- D. He was capable on arrival yesterday
- E. His reasoning about the risks of leaving  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Capacity is shown by what he does with the information for this decision. He understands that leaving could worsen his breathing, appreciates that it could lead to intubation, weighs it against his wish to leave and states a clear choice. Having no dementia, walking steadily and speaking well do not show those abilities. Capacity on arrival does not settle capacity now, because it is assessed at the time of each decision.

*Key feature:* Delirium/agitation 5. Assess decision making capacity in delirium and decide whether a surrogate decision maker or involuntary treatment is needed.

*Source:* Appelbaum PS. Assessment of patients' competence to consent to treatment. New England Journal of Medicine 2007. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMcp074045

#### Q3
**Which of the following is the most appropriate way to respond to his shouting?**

- A. Ask security to stand at his door
- B. Listen and offer a nicotine patch  **(key)**
- C. Remind him that shouting is a crime
- D. Sedate him with lorazepam 1 mg PO
- E. Warn him he will lose his bed

*Explanation:* Verbal de-escalation starts by listening closely to find out what the person wants, here to smoke, and then offering realistic choices, such as a nicotine patch or a supervised break, which acknowledges his need while keeping him safe. Threats about the law or losing his bed are provocative. Posting security at the door of a man who has not threatened anyone escalates the conflict. Sedating a capable, non-violent patient is not justified.

*Key feature:* Delirium/agitation 3. Protect everyone's safety, including staff, families, other patients, and the patient, when managing agitation.

*Source:* Richmond JS, Berlin JS, Fishkind AB, et al. Verbal de-escalation of the agitated patient. Consensus statement of the American Association for Emergency Psychiatry Project BETA De-escalation Workgroup. Western Journal of Emergency Medicine 2012. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22461917/

#### Q4
*Update:* After discussion he still decides to leave against medical advice.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate action as he leaves?**

- A. Ask security to keep him in the department
- B. Call police to return him if he leaves
- C. Document capacity and arrange follow-up  **(key)**
- D. Refuse to give his discharge medications
- E. Tell him he cannot return if he leaves

*Explanation:* A capable adult may refuse treatment, including hospital admission, even when the choice is risky. The physician documents the capacity assessment, the risks discussed and the patient's decision, and still offers the best available alternative care, such as medications, follow-up and an open invitation to return. Detaining him or calling police has no legal basis while he is capable. Withholding medications or barring his return adds harm and breaks the duty of care.

*Key feature:* Delirium/agitation 5. Assess decision making capacity in delirium and decide whether a surrogate decision maker or involuntary treatment is needed.
