### headache-16: A girl sent home from school

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

An 11-year-old girl is brought to the emergency department by her father with a headache that began 6 hours ago at school. The pain is bilateral, frontal and throbbing. She has vomited twice and bright light bothers her. She has had at least six similar but milder headaches in the past year, each lasting several hours and relieved by sleep. Her mother has migraine. She took acetaminophen 500 mg at noon without benefit. She is alert and interactive and prefers to lie in a dark corner. Fundi show sharp disc margins. Cranial nerves, strength, coordination and gait are normal. There is no neck stiffness or rash.

- Temperature: 36.9°C oral
- Pulse: 96/minute
- Resp.: 18/minute
- BP: 104/66 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air
- Weight: 38 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following features of her history most strongly supports a primary headache rather than a secondary cause?**

- A. Bilateral frontal location of the pain
- B. Failure to respond to acetaminophen
- C. Onset of pain during the school day
- D. Recurrent stereotyped attacks  **(key)**
- E. Vomiting twice since the onset

*Explanation:* A stereotyped pattern of at least six similar attacks over a year, each ending with sleep, supports migraine, which by ICHD criteria needs five or more attacks. Bilateral frontal pain is typical of migraine in children, so its location does not separate primary from secondary causes. Vomiting occurs with both migraine and raised intracranial pressure. Onset at school and failure of one acetaminophen dose do not distinguish the two.

*Key feature:* Headache 1. Take a detailed headache history, covering onset, severity, prior episodes, and associated symptoms, to shape the differential.

*Source:* TREKK. Bottom line recommendations: migraine. Version 1.0. 2025. https://trekk.ca/resources/bottom-line-recommendations-migraine

#### Q2
*Update:* She rates the pain as 8 of 10. She is still nauseated and has not kept down oral fluids.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate initial parenteral treatment for her headache?**

- A. dexamethasone 8 mg IV
- B. metoclopramide 5.7 mg IV  **(key)**
- C. morphine 4 mg IV
- D. ondansetron 4 mg IV
- E. 0.9% saline 380 mL IV bolus

*Explanation:* She is vomiting and cannot take oral medication, and IV metoclopramide 0.15 mg/kg, maximum 10 mg, is the recommended first-line emergency treatment for a migraine attack in a child. At 38 kg the dose is 0.15 x 38 = 5.7 mg. Opioids such as morphine are not recommended because of inefficacy and potential harm. IV fluids are given only for dehydration, as a saline bolus alone did not add benefit. Ondansetron does not treat the headache, and there are inadequate data for dexamethasone in children.

*Key feature:* Headache 5. Diagnose and treat migraine when the picture fits and serious pathology has been excluded.

*Source:* Rogers S, Orr SL, Parisien B, Chauvin-Kimoff L. Canadian Paediatric Society position statement. Pharmacological management of acute migraine attacks in children and adolescents presenting to the emergency department. Paediatr Child Health. 2025. https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/acute-migraine

#### Q3
**Which of the following is the most appropriate approach to neuroimaging for this girl today?**

- A. CT head before discharge
- B. CT head with contrast today
- C. MRI brain as an outpatient
- D. MRI brain within 2 weeks
- E. No neuroimaging is indicated  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Her attacks are recurrent and stereotyped, and her fundi, cranial nerves, coordination and gait are normal. The AAN practice parameter advises against routine neuroimaging for children with recurrent headaches and a normal neurological examination. Imaging is reserved for an abnormal examination, a recent change in the headache pattern or features such as seizures. CT with or without contrast adds radiation without benefit here, and an MRI would not change her management.

*Key feature:* Headache 5. Diagnose and treat migraine when the picture fits and serious pathology has been excluded.

*Source:* Lewis DW, Ashwal S, Dahl G, et al. Practice parameter: evaluation of children and adolescents with recurrent headaches. Report of the Quality Standards Subcommittee of the American Academy of Neurology and the Practice Committee of the Child Neurology Society. Neurology. 2002.

#### Q4
*Update:* After treatment her pain is 2 of 10 and she is drinking. Her father asks what to give at home for the next attack.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate home treatment at the onset of her next attack?**

- A. acetaminophen 1 000 mg PO
- B. codeine 30 mg PO
- C. ibuprofen 190 mg PO
- D. ibuprofen 380 mg PO  **(key)**
- E. ibuprofen 760 mg PO

*Explanation:* At 38 kg her ibuprofen dose is 10 x 38 = 380 mg, using 10 mg/kg, maximum 600 mg, taken early in the attack, which is the recommended first-line home treatment for migraine in children. A dose of 190 mg is only 5 mg/kg, and 760 mg is 20 mg/kg, above the 600 mg maximum. Acetaminophen 1 000 mg is about 26 mg/kg, well above the usual 15 mg/kg dose. Opioids such as codeine are not recommended for migraine in children.

*Key feature:* Headache 5. Diagnose and treat migraine when the picture fits and serious pathology has been excluded.

*Source:* Rogers S, Orr SL, Parisien B, Chauvin-Kimoff L. Canadian Paediatric Society position statement. Pharmacological management of acute migraine attacks in children and adolescents presenting to the emergency department. Paediatr Child Health. 2025. https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/acute-migraine

### headache-17: Headache and a whooshing noise

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 26-year-old woman presents to the emergency department with 5 weeks of daily headache that is worse when she lies down and on waking. She hears a whooshing sound in time with her pulse. For 1 week she has had horizontal double vision when looking into the distance, and for a few seconds at a time her vision greys out when she stands up. She has gained 12 kg in the past year. Her only medication is minocycline for acne, started 3 months ago. She is alert and oriented. Pupils are equal and reactive. Limb strength, sensation and coordination are normal.

- Temperature: 36.8°C oral
- Pulse: 82/minute
- Resp.: 16/minute
- BP: 128/78 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air
- Weight: 104 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following symptoms is most specific for raised intracranial pressure?**

- A. Daily bilateral headache
- B. Nausea with the headache
- C. Neck and shoulder stiffness
- D. Photophobia with the headache
- E. Transient visual obscurations  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Transient visual obscurations, brief greying or loss of vision often on standing, are caused by papilledema and point to raised intracranial pressure. With pulsatile tinnitus and horizontal diplopia, they strongly suggest intracranial hypertension. Daily bilateral headache, nausea, photophobia and neck stiffness are common in migraine and tension-type headache and are not specific.

*Key feature:* Headache 1. Take a detailed headache history, covering onset, severity, prior episodes, and associated symptoms, to shape the differential.

*Source:* Mollan SP, Davies B, Silver NC, et al. Idiopathic intracranial hypertension: consensus guidelines on management. J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry. 2018.

#### Q2
**Which of the following examination findings would most strongly support the suspected cause of her symptoms?**

- A. Bilateral optic disc swelling  **(key)**
- B. Bitemporal visual field loss
- C. Gaze-evoked horizontal nystagmus
- D. Ptosis with a dilated pupil
- E. Relative afferent pupillary defect

*Explanation:* Papilledema, bilateral optic disc swelling from raised intracranial pressure, is the key finding in idiopathic intracranial hypertension and must be sought with fundoscopy in any patient with headache and visual symptoms. Bitemporal field loss suggests a lesion compressing the optic chiasm. A relative afferent pupillary defect points to asymmetric optic nerve disease such as optic neuritis. Ptosis with a dilated pupil indicates a third nerve palsy, and gaze-evoked nystagmus suggests a cerebellar or drug cause.

*Key feature:* Headache 3. Examine specifically for red flags such as altered mentation, focal deficits, papilledema, meningismus, or rash.

*Source:* Friedman DI, Liu GT, Digre KB. Revised diagnostic criteria for the pseudotumor cerebri syndrome in adults and children. Neurology. 2013.

#### Q3
*Update:* Fundoscopy shows bilateral disc swelling. She has a left sixth nerve palsy. Non-contrast CT head is normal.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate next investigation for her?**

- A. Carotid Doppler ultrasound
- B. CT angiography of the neck
- C. CT venography of the head  **(key)**
- D. Lumbar puncture while sitting
- E. MRI of the pituitary gland

*Explanation:* Papilledema with a normal non-contrast CT requires venous imaging to exclude cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, which can mimic idiopathic intracranial hypertension, before the diagnosis is made. Lumbar puncture follows imaging and must be done in the lateral decubitus position, because pressure measured sitting is falsely high. CT angiography of the neck and carotid Doppler examine arteries, and a dedicated pituitary MRI does not address the venous differential.

*Key feature:* Headache 6. Investigate a worrying headache promptly with CT or LP, giving empiric therapy first when indicated, as in meningitis.

*Source:* Friedman DI, Liu GT, Digre KB. Revised diagnostic criteria for the pseudotumor cerebri syndrome in adults and children. Neurology. 2013.

#### Q4
*Update:* CT venography is normal. Lumbar puncture in the lateral decubitus position shows an opening pressure of 38 cm H2O with normal CSF cell count, protein and glucose. Visual acuity is normal.

**Which of the following are the most appropriate next steps in her management?**

Select 3.

- A. Acetazolamide PO  **(key)**
- B. Dexamethasone IV
- C. Discontinue minocycline  **(key)**
- D. Hydromorphone IV
- E. Nimodipine PO
- F. Repeat lumbar punctures
- G. Urgent ophthalmology assessment  **(key)**
- H. Ventriculoperitoneal shunt

*Explanation:* An opening pressure of 38 cm H2O, above the adult threshold of 25 cm H2O, with papilledema, normal CSF and normal venous imaging confirms intracranial hypertension. Acetazolamide is first-line medical therapy. Tetracyclines such as minocycline can cause intracranial hypertension and should be stopped. Papilledema threatens vision, so she needs prompt formal visual field and fundus assessment. Serial lumbar punctures, steroids, opioids and nimodipine are not recommended treatments, and a shunt is reserved for rapidly declining vision.

*Key feature:* Headache 2. Consider and exclude dangerous causes such as bleeding, meningitis, mass lesions, and pre-eclampsia when needed.

*Source:* Mollan SP, Davies B, Silver NC, et al. Idiopathic intracranial hypertension: consensus guidelines on management. J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry. 2018.

### headache-18: Night-time eye pain in a pacing man

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 38-year-old man presents to the emergency department at 02:00 with severe pain behind his right eye that woke him 40 minutes ago. He is pacing the room and cannot sit still. He has had similar attacks every night for 9 days, each lasting about an hour, with right eye tearing and a blocked right nostril. A similar run of attacks 2 years ago stopped after 6 weeks. He smokes a pack of cigarettes a day and takes no medications. The right eye is red and tearing, with a small right pupil and mild right ptosis. Visual acuity is normal in both eyes. The rest of the neurological examination is normal.

- Temperature: 36.7°C oral
- Pulse: 94/minute
- Resp.: 18/minute
- BP: 146/88 mmHg
- O2 sat: 98% on room air

#### Q1
**Which of the following features of his history best distinguishes this headache from migraine?**

- A. History of cigarette smoking
- B. Onset during the night
- C. Pain of severe intensity
- D. Restlessness during attacks  **(key)**
- E. Right-sided location of pain

*Explanation:* Restlessness or agitation during attacks is a diagnostic feature of cluster headache in ICHD-3, whereas people with migraine lie still because movement worsens the pain. Severe unilateral pain occurs in both conditions. Migraine can also begin at night. Smoking is common in people with cluster headache but does not separate the two. His attacks of about an hour with ipsilateral tearing and nasal blockage also fit cluster headache.

*Key feature:* Headache 1. Take a detailed headache history, covering onset, severity, prior episodes, and associated symptoms, to shape the differential.

*Source:* Headache Classification Committee of the International Headache Society. The International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition. Cephalalgia. 2018.

#### Q2
**Which of the following alternative diagnoses is most important to exclude in view of his red, painful eye?**

- A. Acute angle-closure glaucoma  **(key)**
- B. Bacterial conjunctivitis
- C. Diffuse episcleritis
- D. Subconjunctival hemorrhage
- E. Viral conjunctivitis

*Explanation:* A red, painful eye with severe periorbital headache must prompt exclusion of acute angle-closure glaucoma, which can cost vision within hours. Here the small right pupil, normal visual acuity and stereotyped nightly attacks with tearing and nasal blockage favour cluster headache, whereas glaucoma typically gives a mid-dilated, poorly reactive pupil and reduced acuity. Conjunctivitis, episcleritis and subconjunctival hemorrhage do not cause severe headache with autonomic features.

*Key feature:* Headache 4. When an intracranial cause is unlikely, consider sources like temporal arteritis, glaucoma, zoster, or sinusitis.

*Source:* Walls RM, Hockberger RS, Gausche-Hill M, Erickson TB, Wilcox SR, editors. Rosen's Emergency Medicine: Concepts and Clinical Practice. 10th ed. Elsevier. 2023. Chapter 18, Red and Painful Eye. https://www.us.elsevierhealth.com/rosens-emergency-medicine-concepts-and-clinical-practice-9780323757898.html

#### Q3
*Update:* Intraocular pressure is normal in both eyes. He is still in severe pain.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate first treatment for his current attack?**

- A. hydromorphone 1 mg IV
- B. ketorolac 30 mg IV
- C. oxygen 12 L/minute by mask  **(key)**
- D. prednisone 60 mg PO
- E. verapamil 80 mg PO

*Explanation:* His attack is in progress and severe, and high-flow oxygen, 12 L/minute or more by non-rebreather mask, has a Level A recommendation for aborting a cluster attack and has no cardiovascular contraindications. Subcutaneous sumatriptan is the other first-line acute option. Prednisone and verapamil are used to shorten or prevent a cluster period, not to stop an attack in progress. Opioids and ketorolac are not established acute treatments for cluster headache.

*Key feature:* Headache 5. Diagnose and treat migraine when the picture fits and serious pathology has been excluded.

*Source:* Robbins MS, Starling AJ, Pringsheim TM, Becker WJ, Schwedt TJ. Treatment of cluster headache: the American Headache Society evidence-based guidelines. Headache. 2016.

- B. codeine 60 mg PO
- C. ibuprofen 600 mg PO
- D. rizatriptan 10 mg PO
- E. sumatriptan 6 mg SC  **(key)**

*Explanation:* His attacks last about an hour and he has no coronary or cerebrovascular disease, so subcutaneous sumatriptan 6 mg, which has a Level A recommendation for acute cluster headache and works within minutes, is suitable. Oral triptans such as rizatriptan act too slowly for attacks this short. Acetaminophen, ibuprofen and codeine are not effective acute treatments for cluster attacks.

*Key feature:* Headache 5. Diagnose and treat migraine when the picture fits and serious pathology has been excluded.

### headache-19: Headache two days after a test

A 31-year-old woman presents to the emergency department with a headache that began 36 hours after a lumbar puncture done in this department to investigate a sudden headache. That workup was negative and the first headache resolved. The new headache is bilateral and occipital. It starts within 15 minutes of sitting or standing and eases within minutes of lying flat. She has neck ache and muffled hearing when upright. She has no fever, vomiting or visual change. She has taken acetaminophen and ibuprofen. She is lying flat, alert and comfortable. **Which of the following features of her history most strongly supports a post-dural puncture headache?**

- A. Absence of fever or vomiting
- B. Bilateral occipital location
- C. Negative prior workup for bleeding
- D. Relief within minutes of lying flat  **(key)**
- E. Use of simple analgesics at home

*Explanation:* The defining feature of post-dural puncture headache is its postural pattern, worsening soon after sitting or standing and easing on lying flat, from low CSF pressure after a dural leak. Onset within 5 days of the puncture and hearing change when upright also fit. Occipital location, the absence of fever and analgesic use are nonspecific. *Key feature:* Headache 1. Take a detailed headache history, covering onset, severity, prior episodes, and associated symptoms, to shape the differential.

- A. Headache no longer postural  **(key)**
- B. Hearing change when upright
- C. Nausea when sitting up
- D. Neck stiffness when upright
- E. Onset within 3 days of the puncture

*Explanation:* A headache after dural puncture that loses its postural pattern, or new focal deficits, seizures, visual change or reduced consciousness, should prompt neuroimaging for complications such as subdural hematoma or cerebral venous thrombosis. Hearing change, nausea and neck stiffness on sitting up are recognized features of low CSF pressure. Onset within 5 days of the puncture is typical.

*Key feature:* Headache 3. Examine specifically for red flags such as altered mentation, focal deficits, papilledema, meningismus, or rash.

*Source:* Uppal V, Russell R, Sondekoppam R, et al. Consensus practice guidelines on postdural puncture headache from a multisociety, international working group: a summary report. JAMA Netw Open. 2023.

- B. Extra oral fluids afterward
- C. Prone positioning afterward
- D. Use of a pencil-point needle  **(key)**
- E. Wider bore cutting needle

*Explanation:* Needle design is the main modifiable risk factor for her complication: atraumatic pencil-point needles substantially reduce post-dural puncture headache compared with conventional cutting needles, with similar success rates, in a meta-analysis of randomized trials. A wider bore cutting needle leaves a larger dural hole and increases the risk. Bed rest, prone positioning and extra fluids after the procedure do not prevent this headache.

*Key feature:* Headache 6. Investigate a worrying headache promptly with CT or LP, giving empiric therapy first when indicated, as in meningitis.

### headache-20: Sudden headache and double vision

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 57-year-old man presents to the emergency department with a sudden severe frontal headache that began 5 hours ago while he was watching television. He has vomited four times and reports blurred and double vision. He has hypertension treated with amlodipine and has had erectile dysfunction and fatigue for a year. He is drowsy but oriented. His left eyelid droops and the left eye rests down and out, with a dilated left pupil. He cannot see the examiner's fingers in the temporal half of the visual field of either eye. Neck flexion is mildly painful. Limb strength is normal.

- Temperature: 37.4°C oral
- Pulse: 104/minute
- Resp.: 18/minute
- BP: 92/58 mmHg
- O2 sat: 97% on room air
- Weight: 86 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following examination findings most specifically points to a lesion in the sellar region?**

- A. Drowsiness with intact orientation
- B. Left third nerve palsy
- C. Low blood pressure
- D. Pain on neck flexion
- E. Temporal field loss in both eyes  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Loss of the temporal fields in both eyes is a bitemporal hemianopia from compression of the optic chiasm, which lies directly above the pituitary. With sudden headache, vomiting and ophthalmoplegia it points to pituitary apoplexy. A third nerve palsy also occurs with a posterior communicating artery aneurysm, so it localizes less specifically. Drowsiness, neck pain and low BP occur with subarachnoid blood and adrenal insufficiency but do not localize the lesion.

*Key feature:* Headache 3. Examine specifically for red flags such as altered mentation, focal deficits, papilledema, meningismus, or rash.

*Source:* Rajasekaran S, Vanderpump M, Baldeweg S, et al. UK guidelines for the management of pituitary apoplexy. Clin Endocrinol (Oxf). 2011.

#### Q2
**Which of the following features of his history most suggests a pre-existing pituitary tumour?**

- A. Double vision since onset
- B. Erectile dysfunction and fatigue  **(key)**
- C. Hypertension on amlodipine
- D. Onset of headache at rest
- E. Repeated vomiting since onset

*Explanation:* A year of erectile dysfunction and fatigue suggests pituitary hormone deficiency from a pre-existing macroadenoma, the usual substrate for apoplexy. Double vision and vomiting are acute effects of the bleed rather than clues to a long-standing tumour. Onset at rest and treated hypertension do not point to a pituitary cause.

*Key feature:* Headache 1. Take a detailed headache history, covering onset, severity, prior episodes, and associated symptoms, to shape the differential.

*Source:* Rajasekaran S, Vanderpump M, Baldeweg S, et al. UK guidelines for the management of pituitary apoplexy. Clin Endocrinol (Oxf). 2011.

#### Q3
*Update:* Non-contrast CT head shows a 2.5 cm hyperdense sellar mass extending upward. There is no subarachnoid blood. Na 131 mmol/L. Glucose 4.1 mmol/L.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate immediate medication for him?**

- A. cabergoline 0.5 mg PO
- B. dexamethasone 10 mg IV
- C. hydrocortisone 100 mg IV  **(key)**
- D. levothyroxine 100 mcg IV
- E. nimodipine 60 mg PO

*Explanation:* Hypotension, drowsiness, hyponatremia and a hemorrhagic sellar mass suggest acute adrenal insufficiency from pituitary apoplexy. The UK guideline advises prompt IV hydrocortisone in this setting, which may be life saving, without waiting for cortisol results. *Key feature:* Headache 1. Take a detailed headache history, covering onset, severity, prior episodes, and associated symptoms, to shape the differential.

*Source:* Searchlight Pharma Inc. Nardil (phenelzine sulfate tablets). Canadian product monograph. 2022. https://searchlightpharma.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Nardil-PM-ENG-Oct2022.pdf

#### Q2
*Update:* Twenty minutes later his blood pressure is 136/84 mmHg. He says the pain matches his usual attacks in site, quality and associated symptoms.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment for his headache now?**

- A. acetaminophen 1 000 mg IV
- B. dexamethasone 10 mg IV
- C. ketorolac 30 mg IV  **(key)**
- D. magnesium sulfate 2 g IV
- E. morphine 5 mg IV

*Explanation:* His headache matches his usual migraine, his blood pressure has settled and his examination is normal, so migraine can be treated. The Canadian Headache Society emergency review strongly recommends ketorolac for migraine pain, and he has no kidney disease, peptic ulcer or asthma. The same review recommends against dexamethasone for pain relief, and weakly against acetaminophen, magnesium sulfate and morphine. He has also already failed oral acetaminophen today.

*Key feature:* Headache 5. Diagnose and treat migraine when the picture fits and serious pathology has been excluded.

#### Q3
**Which of the following should he take at the start of future attacks while he takes phenelzine?**

- A. butalbital compound PO
- B. naproxen 500 mg PO  **(key)**
- C. rizatriptan 10 mg PO
- D. sumatriptan 100 mg PO
- E. tramadol with acetaminophen PO

*Explanation:* The Canadian Headache Society guideline gives naproxen sodium a strong recommendation with high quality evidence for acute migraine, and it does not interact with his phenelzine. The same guideline advises avoiding sumatriptan and rizatriptan within 14 days of a monoamine oxidase inhibitor, because both are cleared by monoamine oxidase A. Canadian product monographs for both drugs list this combination as a contraindication, so his usual sumatriptan must stop while he takes phenelzine. *Key feature:* Headache 5. Diagnose and treat migraine when the picture fits and serious pathology has been excluded.
