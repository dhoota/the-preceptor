You are working in a community hospital emergency department. A 38-year-old man presents to the emergency department with a droopy right face that he noticed while brushing his teeth this morning. Last night he had aching behind his right ear. Sounds seem loud in his right ear and food tastes odd. He has no headache, limb weakness, numbness or speech difficulty. He has no medical history and takes no medications. He cannot wrinkle the right forehead or raise the right eyebrow. The right eye closes fully, but only with effort. The right nasolabial fold is flat and the right corner of the mouth droops. Speech, limb strength, sensation and coordination are normal.

- Temperature: 36.8°C oral
- Pulse: 76/minute
- Resp.: 14/minute

#### Q1
**Which of the following examination findings best separates a peripheral facial nerve palsy from a stroke in this patient?**

- A. Asymmetric smile when asked
- B. Drooling from the lower lip
- C. Drooping of the mouth corner
- D. Flattened nasolabial fold
- E. Weakness of the forehead muscles  **(key)**

*Explanation:* The forehead receives upper motor neuron input from both hemispheres, so a stroke above the facial nucleus usually spares it. He cannot wrinkle the right forehead or raise the right eyebrow, which places the lesion in the facial nerve itself. A drooping mouth corner, a flat nasolabial fold, an asymmetric smile and drooling occur with both central and peripheral lesions, so none of them separates the two. His normal limb, sensory and speech examination fits an isolated facial nerve palsy.

*Key feature:* Cerebrovascular accident (CVA) 1. For acute neurologic deficits, consider stroke mimics such as hypoglycemia, migraine, Todd's paralysis, or tumour.

*Source:* de Almeida JR and colleagues. Management of Bell palsy: clinical practice guideline. CMAJ. 2014. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24934895/

- B. Pain behind the right ear
- C. Pronator drift of the right arm  **(key)**
- D. Reduced tearing of the right eye
- E. Sound sensitivity in the right ear

*Explanation:* A pronator drift is a sign of corticospinal tract dysfunction and points to a lesion in the brain rather than the facial nerve. Pain behind the ear is common early in Bell palsy, and altered taste, sound sensitivity and reduced tearing come from branches of the facial nerve along its course, so all four support a peripheral palsy. Any limb, sensory or speech deficit should prompt stroke assessment instead of a diagnosis of Bell palsy.

*Key feature:* Cerebrovascular accident (CVA) 1. For acute neurologic deficits, consider stroke mimics such as hypoglycemia, migraine, Todd's paralysis, or tumour.

*Source:* de Almeida JR and colleagues. Management of Bell palsy: clinical practice guideline. CMAJ. 2014. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24934895/

Medications: apixaban 5 mg twice daily and amlodipine. Her last apixaban dose was at 08:00 and it is now 14:00. Capillary glucose is 6.2 mmol/L. GCS is 14 (E4 V4 M6). **Which of the following features of her presentation favour a subdural hematoma over an acute ischemic stroke?**

- D. Normal capillary glucose

*Explanation:* Ischemic stroke causes a deficit that is maximal at onset or fluctuates over hours. - D. Normal speech and language

*Explanation:* Functional weakness is diagnosed by positive signs, and an outstretched arm that drifts without pronating is one of them, because organic weakness makes the arm pronate as it drifts.
