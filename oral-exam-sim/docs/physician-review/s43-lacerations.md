### lacerations-16: Forearm through a storm door

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 27-year-old man presents to the emergency department 1 hour after his right forearm went through the glass panel of a storm door that had jammed. He is a right-handed electrician. He has a 3 cm transverse laceration on the ulnar side of the volar wrist, 2 cm proximal to the wrist crease. A bystander held pressure with a towel. He is healthy and takes no medications. He completed his childhood immunizations.

- Temperature: 36.8°C oral
- Pulse: 96/minute
- Resp.: 16/minute
- BP: 132/78 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air
- Weight: 82 kg

#### Q1
*Update:* He cannot cross his index and middle fingers. Light touch is reduced over the little finger and the ulnar half of the ring finger. Flexion of all fingers is full.

**Which of the following structures is most likely injured, given these examination findings?**

- A. Anterior interosseous nerve
- B. Flexor carpi ulnaris tendon
- C. Median nerve at the wrist
- D. Superficial radial nerve
- E. Ulnar nerve at the wrist  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Inability to cross the index and middle fingers means the interossei are weak, and sensory loss over the little finger and ulnar ring finger matches ulnar nerve territory. At the wrist the ulnar nerve runs beside the ulnar artery under the flexor carpi ulnaris. The median nerve supplies sensation to the thumb, index and middle finger pads. The anterior interosseous nerve is purely motor and branches in the proximal forearm. The superficial radial nerve supplies the dorsal radial hand. A cut flexor carpi ulnaris tendon weakens wrist flexion but causes no sensory loss.

*Key feature:* Lacerations 2. Examine for damage to nearby tendons, nerves, vessels, and joints in patients with lacerations.

*Source:* Dy CJ, Mackinnon SE. Ulnar neuropathy: evaluation and management. Curr Rev Musculoskelet Med. 2016. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12178-016-9327-x

#### Q2
*Update:* When the towel is lifted, dark blood oozes steadily from the wound. The hand is warm with a capillary refill of 2 seconds.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate first step to control the bleeding from this wound?**

- A. Arterial tourniquet on the upper arm
- B. Blind clamping with a hemostat
- C. Direct pressure over the wound  **(key)**
- D. Elevation of the arm alone
- E. Suture ligation through the skin

*Explanation:* Firm direct pressure over the wound controls most bleeding from an extremity laceration and is the first step. A tourniquet is kept for bleeding that pressure cannot control, and this ooze is not life threatening. Blind clamping or suture ligation in a bloody field can crush or tie the ulnar nerve, which lies beside the ulnar artery at this level, and can ruin a vessel that needs repair. Elevation helps but does not stop bleeding on its own.

*Key feature:* Lacerations 2. Examine for damage to nearby tendons, nerves, vessels, and joints in patients with lacerations.

*Source:* Roberts JR, Custalow CB, Thomsen TW, editors. Roberts and Hedges' Clinical Procedures in Emergency Medicine and Acute Care. 7th ed. Elsevier. 2019. Chapter 34, Principles of Wound Management. https://shop.elsevier.com/books/roberts-and-hedges-clinical-procedures-in-emergency-medicine-and-acute-care/roberts/978-0-323-35478-3

#### Q3
*Update:* Pressure controls the bleeding. Radiographs show no foreign body. The on call hand surgeon agrees to see him in clinic this week.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate management of this wound in the emergency department today?**

- A. Close the skin and review in clinic in 3 weeks
- B. Explore and repair the nerve in the department
- C. Leave the wound open and review in 48 hours
- D. Skin closure, splint, hand surgery referral  **(key)**
- E. Start cephalexin and review in clinic in 1 week

*Explanation:* A cut ulnar nerve at the wrist is repaired by a hand surgeon, and direct repair of the nerve ends is the standard of care when it is possible. The emergency physician irrigates the wound, closes the skin, splints the wrist and arranges the early review the surgeon has offered. Nerve repair is an operating room procedure, not an emergency department one. Waiting 3 weeks lets the ends retract and scar, which makes direct repair harder. A clean glass wound seen at 1 hour does not need to be left open, and antibiotics do not replace repair.

*Key feature:* Lacerations 3. Before closing a high risk wound, explore, irrigate, and image as needed, and consider delayed or no closure.

*Source:* Griffin JW, Hogan MV, Chhabra AB, Deal DN. Peripheral nerve repair and reconstruction. J Bone Joint Surg Am. 2013. https://doi.org/10.2106/JBJS.L.00704

### lacerations-17: Boy caught under a pasture fence

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 5-year-old boy is brought to the emergency department by his parents 3 hours after he slid under a barbed wire fence in a cattle pasture on the family farm. He has a 4 cm ragged laceration on the lateral left thigh with dried mud and manure in the wound. He has no other injuries. His parents chose not to vaccinate him, and he has received no vaccines. He is otherwise healthy.

He is alert and crying. Distal pulses, sensation and movement of the leg are normal. The wound edges look viable.

- Temperature: 37.1°C tympanic
- Pulse: 118/minute
- Resp.: 24/minute
- BP: 104/62 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air
- Weight: 18 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most appropriate tetanus prophylaxis for this boy at this visit?**

- A. DTaP-IPV and immune globulin  **(key)**
- B. DTaP-IPV vaccine alone
- C. Tdap and immune globulin
- D. Tdap vaccine alone
- E. Tetanus immune globulin alone

*Explanation:* He has had no tetanus doses, and a wound soiled with mud and manure is not clean and minor. Under the Canadian Immunization Guide he needs both a tetanus toxoid vaccine and tetanus immune globulin today. Children under 7 years receive DTaP-IPV, not the adolescent and adult Tdap formulation. Vaccine alone takes weeks to protect, and immune globulin alone gives no lasting immunity. His primary series should then be completed.

*Key feature:* Lacerations 4. Give antibiotics and close follow-up for infection prone wounds, but withhold antibiotics from low risk ones.

*Source:* Public Health Agency of Canada. Canadian Immunization Guide. Part 4. Tetanus toxoid. Tetanus prophylaxis in wound management. Updated 2026. https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/publications/healthy-living/canadian-immunization-guide-part-4-active-vaccines/page-22-tetanus-toxoid.html

#### Q2
**Which of the following is the most appropriate way to give the vaccine and the immune globulin to him?**

- A. Both in one syringe into the same thigh
- B. Immune globulin infiltrated around the wound
- C. Immune globulin IV and the vaccine IM
- D. Separate syringes at separate sites  **(key)**
- E. Vaccine deferred 4 weeks after globulin

*Explanation:* The Canadian Immunization Guide advises that tetanus toxoid vaccine and tetanus immune globulin given together go in at different injection sites with separate needles and syringes, which avoids local interference with his response to the vaccine. Tetanus immune globulin is a deep IM injection. Unlike rabies immune globulin, it is not infiltrated around the wound, and it is not given IV. Mixing both in one syringe or one site risks blunting the vaccine. Deferring the vaccine for 4 weeks delays the first dose of the primary series he needs today.

*Key feature:* Lacerations 4. Give antibiotics and close follow-up for infection prone wounds, but withhold antibiotics from low risk ones.

*Source:* Public Health Agency of Canada. Canadian Immunization Guide. Part 4. Tetanus toxoid. Tetanus prophylaxis in wound management. Updated 2026. https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/publications/healthy-living/canadian-immunization-guide-part-4-active-vaccines/page-22-tetanus-toxoid.html

#### Q3
**Which of the following is the most appropriate way to clean this wound before closure?**

- A. Gentle swabbing with saline gauze
- B. Hydrogen peroxide irrigation of the wound
- C. Povidone iodine soak for 20 minutes
- D. Scrubbing with full strength chlorhexidine
- E. Syringe irrigation and removal of debris  **(key)**

*Explanation:* Mud and manure carry a heavy bacterial load and tetanus spores, so the wound needs pressure irrigation, for example with a 30 to 60 mL syringe and a splash shield, and removal of visible debris. Swabbing moves debris around without reaching the depth of a ragged wound. Hydrogen peroxide, full strength chlorhexidine and a povidone iodine soak injure tissue inside the wound and do not replace mechanical cleaning.

*Key feature:* Lacerations 3. Before closing a high risk wound, explore, irrigate, and image as needed, and consider delayed or no closure.

*Source:* Roberts JR, Custalow CB, Thomsen TW, editors. Roberts and Hedges' Clinical Procedures in Emergency Medicine and Acute Care. 7th ed. Elsevier. 2019. Chapter 34, Principles of Wound Management. https://shop.elsevier.com/books/roberts-and-hedges-clinical-procedures-in-emergency-medicine-and-acute-care/roberts/978-0-323-35478-3

#### Q4
*Update:* After irrigation the wound is clean with viable edges. It is closed with simple sutures.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate follow-up plan for this wound?**

- A. Family doctor review in 2 weeks
- B. Plastic surgery clinic in 1 week
- C. Recheck of the wound in 2 days  **(key)**
- D. Return only for suture removal
- E. Suture removal in 3 days

*Explanation:* A ragged wound contaminated with manure is infection prone, so a recheck in about 2 days catches early infection while it is easy to treat. Parents should also learn the signs of infection, such as spreading redness, pus, fever or increasing pain. Review at 2 weeks or only at suture removal misses that window. Thigh sutures usually stay in for 7 to 14 days, so removal at 3 days risks dehiscence. A plastic surgery clinic adds nothing for a closed thigh wound.

*Key feature:* Lacerations 4. Give antibiotics and close follow-up for infection prone wounds, but withhold antibiotics from low risk ones.

*Source:* Roberts JR, Custalow CB, Thomsen TW, editors. Roberts and Hedges' Clinical Procedures in Emergency Medicine and Acute Care. 7th ed. Elsevier. 2019. Chapter 34, Principles of Wound Management. https://shop.elsevier.com/books/roberts-and-hedges-clinical-procedures-in-emergency-medicine-and-acute-care/roberts/978-0-323-35478-3

### lacerations-18: Teenager with forearm cuts

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 15-year-old girl is brought to the emergency department by her mother, who found blood on her sleeve. The girl says she cut her forearm on a broken mirror last night. On the volar left forearm there are six parallel, evenly spaced linear cuts, each 3 to 4 cm long. Older healed linear scars lie beside them. The deepest cut gapes 3 mm and exposes subcutaneous fat. The edges are clean. She has no medical history, takes no medications and her immunizations are up to date.

She avoids eye contact. Hand sensation, strength and capillary refill are normal.

- Temperature: 36.8°C oral
- Pulse: 92/minute
- Resp.: 16/minute
- BP: 112/68 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air
- Weight: 54 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in clarifying how these wounds happened?**

- A. Accept the mirror account and repair
- B. Ask her alone about self-harm and suicide  **(key)**
- C. Ask her mother to describe how it happened
- D. Order a urine drug screen first
- E. Refer to outpatient counselling only

*Explanation:* Parallel, evenly spaced cuts of similar length beside older healed linear scars fit repeated self-injury, not a single accident with glass. She should be asked in private, without her mother present, about self-harm, suicidal thoughts, intent and plans. Accepting the mirror account misses that risk. Her mother can give useful collateral later, but she did not see the injury and her presence may stop an honest answer. A drug screen does not replace asking. Counselling referral without a risk assessment may miss active suicidal intent.

*Key feature:* Lacerations 1. Clarify how every laceration happened to identify wounds at risk of infection, foreign body, or deep structure injury.

*Source:* Korczak DJ, Canadian Paediatric Society Mental Health and Developmental Disabilities Committee. Suicidal ideation and behaviour. Paediatr Child Health. 2015. Reaffirmed 2024. https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/suicidal-ideation-and-behaviour

#### Q2
*Update:* She tells you she cut herself with a razor blade about 12 hours ago. She denies suicidal intent.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate management of the gaping cut, given that it is 12 hours old?**

- A. Cephalexin for 5 days and close now
- B. Close after a dose of cefazolin IV
- C. Close primarily after irrigation  **(key)**
- D. Leave open to heal on its own
- E. Pack and close in 3 to 5 days

*Explanation:* A clean, linear razor cut on the forearm of a healthy teenager can be closed primarily after irrigation, even 12 hours after injury. In a large emergency department cohort, infection was no more common in lacerations closed more than 12 hours after injury, and the main risks were diabetes, a lower limb site, contamination and length over 5 cm. She has none of these. Leaving the wound open or delaying closure prolongs healing and widens the scar. Oral or IV antibiotics add nothing in a clean, low risk wound.

*Key feature:* Lacerations 3. Before closing a high risk wound, explore, irrigate, and image as needed, and consider delayed or no closure.

*Source:* Quinn JV, Polevoi SK, Kohn MA. Traumatic lacerations: what are the risks for infection and has the 'golden period' of laceration care disappeared? Emerg Med J. 2014. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23314208/

#### Q3
*Update:* She has cut herself about once a week for 4 months. She denies suicidal thoughts or plans. Her mother is supportive.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate step before she leaves the emergency department?**

- A. Admission to an adolescent inpatient unit
- B. Discharge with a pamphlet on self-harm
- C. Involuntary hold under mental health law
- D. Psychosocial assessment by mental health staff  **(key)**
- E. Urgent referral to her family doctor only

*Explanation:* Repeated self-harm over 4 months calls for a psychosocial assessment by a mental health clinician before discharge, covering risk, needs and a safety plan made with her and her family. Denying suicidal thoughts does not remove the need for that assessment. Written material or a family doctor referral alone skips it. She has no suicidal intent or plan and a supportive parent, so an involuntary hold or admission is not justified on these facts.

*Key feature:* Suicide risk 4. For low risk suicidal patients, agree on an outpatient plan together and discharge at the right time.

*Source:* National Institute for Health and Care Excellence. Self-harm: assessment, management and preventing recurrence. NICE guideline NG225. 2022. https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng225

### lacerations-19: Angler with a thumb injury

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

You are working in a rural hospital emergency department in northern Manitoba. A 58-year-old man presents 2 hours after a large single barbed fishing hook buried itself in the pad of his left thumb while he was unhooking a pike in his boat on a lake. The hook entered deeply. The point and barb can be felt just under the skin near the radial side of the pad. He has type 2 diabetes treated with metformin. His last tetanus booster was 3 years ago.

The thumb is warm with normal capillary refill. Sensation on both sides of the pad and thumb movement are normal.

- Temperature: 36.7°C oral
- Pulse: 84/minute
- Resp.: 16/minute
- BP: 138/84 mmHg
- O2 sat: 98% on room air
- Weight: 92 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most appropriate method to remove this hook after local anesthesia?**

- A. Advance and cut technique  **(key)**
- B. Plain radiograph before removal
- C. Referral to a hand surgeon
- D. Retrograde removal technique
- E. String yank technique

*Explanation:* The hook entered deeply and its point and barb lie just under the skin, which suits the advance and cut technique: push the point out through the skin, cut off the barb, then back the shank out. Retrograde removal works only for barbless or very superficial hooks, because the barb catches tissue on the way out. The string yank technique suits superficially embedded hooks. A radiograph adds nothing when the whole hook is visible or palpable. With normal sensation, movement and perfusion, a hand surgeon is not needed.

*Key feature:* Lacerations 3. Before closing a high risk wound, explore, irrigate, and image as needed, and consider delayed or no closure.

*Source:* Roberts JR, Custalow CB, Thomsen TW, editors. Roberts and Hedges' Clinical Procedures in Emergency Medicine and Acute Care. 7th ed. Elsevier. 2019. Chapter 36, Foreign Body Removal. https://shop.elsevier.com/books/roberts-and-hedges-clinical-procedures-in-emergency-medicine-and-acute-care/roberts/978-0-323-35478-3

#### Q2
*Update:* He returns 3 days later with rapidly spreading redness and swelling from the thumb to the mid forearm. There is no crepitus and his pain matches the findings. Temperature is 38.6°C.

**Which of the following empiric antibiotic regimens is most appropriate, given where this wound was acquired?**

- A. cefazolin 2 g IV every 8 hours
- B. ceftriaxone 2 g IV plus doxycycline 100 mg IV  **(key)**
- C. clindamycin 900 mg IV every 8 hours
- D. penicillin G 4 million units plus clindamycin 900 mg IV
- E. vancomycin 15 mg/kg IV every 12 hours

*Explanation:* A fast spreading infection within days of a wound sustained in fresh lake water raises concern for Aeromonas hydrophila, a gram negative rod found in fresh water. IDSA 2014 recommends doxycycline plus either ciprofloxacin or ceftriaxone, and ceftriaxone also covers streptococci and susceptible staphylococci. Cefazolin, clindamycin and vancomycin target gram positive skin flora and do not reliably cover Aeromonas. Penicillin with clindamycin is aimed at streptococcal and clostridial infection and misses Aeromonas, which often makes beta lactamases.

*Key feature:* Lacerations 1. Clarify how every laceration happened to identify wounds at risk of infection, foreign body, or deep structure injury.

*Source:* Stevens DL, Bisno AL, Chambers HF, and colleagues. Practice guidelines for the diagnosis and management of skin and soft tissue infections: 2014 update by the Infectious Diseases Society of America. Clin Infect Dis. 2014. https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/59/2/e10/2895845

#### Q3
*Update:* The thumb pad is tense, swollen and exquisitely tender. Bedside ultrasound shows a 1 cm fluid collection in the pulp beneath the entry wound.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate next step for the thumb, in addition to IV antibiotics?**

- A. Aspiration of the pulp with a needle
- B. Hot soaks and elevation of the hand
- C. Removal of the thumbnail
- D. Repeat ultrasound in 48 hours
- E. Surgical drainage of the pulp space  **(key)**

*Explanation:* A tense pad with a fluid collection is a felon, an abscess within the closed, septated compartments of the fingertip pulp, and it needs surgical drainage as well as antibiotics. Pus under pressure there can cause necrosis of the pulp and osteomyelitis of the distal phalanx. Needle aspiration does not decompress the separate compartments. Soaks and elevation do not drain pus. Nail removal treats pus under the nail, not in the pulp. Waiting 48 hours for another scan delays drainage.

*Key feature:* Lacerations 3. Before closing a high risk wound, explore, irrigate, and image as needed, and consider delayed or no closure.

*Source:* Clark DC. Common acute hand infections. Am Fam Physician. 2003. https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2003/1201/p2167.html

### lacerations-20: Preschooler who fell from a slide

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 3-year-old girl is brought to the emergency department by her father 1 hour after she fell forward from the top of a playground slide and struck her chin on the ground. She cried at once and did not lose consciousness. She has a 2 cm horizontal laceration under the chin. The edges come together easily without tension, and there is no debris. She is healthy and fully immunized.

She is consolable but keeps holding her jaw. She moves her neck freely without pain. There are no other injuries.

- Temperature: 37.0°C tympanic
- Pulse: 124/minute
- Resp.: 26/minute
- BP: 98/60 mmHg
- O2 sat: 99% on room air
- Weight: 15 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following associated injuries is most important to look for in this child?**

- A. Cervical spine ligament injury
- B. Mandibular condyle fracture  **(key)**
- C. Nasal septal hematoma
- D. Orbital floor fracture
- E. Zygomatic arch fracture

*Explanation:* A blow to the point of the chin drives the mandible backward and transmits the force to the condyles. The condyle is often involved when a child's mandible breaks, and under 5 years of age these fractures are uncommon and mostly follow falls. Holding her jaw is a clue. Look for pain in front of the ear, malocclusion, deviation on opening and limited opening. She moves her neck freely without pain, which makes a cervical injury unlikely. Nasal, orbital floor and zygomatic injuries follow blows to the midface, not the chin.

*Key feature:* Lacerations 2. Examine for damage to nearby tendons, nerves, vessels, and joints in patients with lacerations.

*Source:* Bottini GB, Roccia F, Sobrero F. Management of pediatric mandibular condyle fractures: a literature review. J Clin Med. 2024. https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm13226921

#### Q2
*Update:* She has tenderness in front of the right ear. Her jaw deviates to the right on opening, and her teeth do not meet normally.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate next step for these findings?**

- A. CT of the mandible  **(key)**
- B. Dental clinic review in 1 week
- C. Discharge on a soft diet
- D. Skull radiograph series
- E. Ultrasound of the jaw

*Explanation:* Pain in front of the ear, deviation toward that side on opening and malocclusion point to a condylar fracture. CT shows the condyles clearly, and a panoramic film alone is now considered inadequate to assess them. A missed condylar fracture in a young child can lead to ankylosis of the joint, facial deformity or malocclusion, so it needs a diagnosis and surgical follow-up. Skull films show the condyles poorly. Ultrasound is not a reliable test for this fracture. Discharge or dental review without imaging leaves the fracture undiagnosed.

*Key feature:* Lacerations 2. Examine for damage to nearby tendons, nerves, vessels, and joints in patients with lacerations.

*Source:* Bottini GB, Roccia F, Sobrero F. Management of pediatric mandibular condyle fractures: a literature review. J Clin Med. 2024. https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm13226921

#### Q3
*Update:* CT shows a nondisplaced right condylar fracture. Oral and maxillofacial surgery will see her tomorrow. You close the chin wound with tissue adhesive.

**Which of the following instructions about the tissue adhesive is most appropriate to give her father?**

- A. Allow brief wetting but no soaking  **(key)**
- B. Apply petroleum jelly over the glue daily
- C. Keep the wound fully dry for 7 days
- D. Peel the glue off after 3 days
- E. Return in 7 days for glue removal

*Explanation:* Tissue adhesive tolerates brief wetting, such as a quick rinse or shower, but soaking in a bath or swimming loosens it early. Ointments and petroleum jelly dissolve the adhesive and should not be applied over it. The wound does not need to stay fully dry.
