### pulmonary-edema-16: Breathless in front of the television

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 71-year-old man presents to the emergency department with 90 minutes of worsening shortness of breath that began while he was watching television. He has hypertension, type 2 diabetes and benign prostatic hyperplasia. He takes amlodipine, ramipril, metformin, a second diabetes tablet he cannot name, and tadalafil 5 mg daily for his prostate, last taken 9 hours ago. He takes no diuretic. He is sweaty, sitting upright and cannot finish a sentence, but he is alert and follows commands. The JVP is 5 cm above the sternal angle. There are crackles to the mid zones of both lungs and trace ankle edema.

- Temperature: 36.9°C oral
- Pulse: 118/minute
- Resp.: 34/minute
- BP: 194/108 mmHg
- O2 sat: 85% on 10 L/minute by face mask

#### Q1
*Update:* Noninvasive ventilation is started. After 10 minutes his BP is 188/104 mmHg and his saturation is 89%.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate medication to give him next?**

- A. furosemide 40 mg IV  **(key)**
- B. labetalol 20 mg IV
- C. morphine 2 mg IV
- D. nitroglycerin 20 mcg/minute IV
- E. nitroglycerin 0.4 mg sublingual

*Explanation:* Tadalafil taken 9 hours ago rules out any nitrate, whether sublingual or a low dose infusion. Kloner found the fall in BP with nitroglycerin was still greater at 24 hours after tadalafil and had gone by 48 hours. Noninvasive ventilation already lowers his preload and afterload. He is congested and takes no diuretic, and the ESC guideline suggests furosemide 20 to 40 mg IV as the starting dose for a diuretic naive patient. Labetalol lowers contractility during acute pulmonary edema and can precipitate shock. Morphine is not recommended routinely and is linked to more intubation.

*Key feature:* Pulmonary edema 1. Treat pulmonary edema early and aggressively, such as with BiPAP, before the cause is known.

*Source:* Kloner RA, Hutter AM, Emmick JT, et al. Time course of the interaction between tadalafil and nitrates. J Am Coll Cardiol. 2003.

#### Q2
*Update:* After 40 minutes on noninvasive ventilation his BP is 158/92 mmHg, his pulse is 98/minute and his saturation is 94%. His daughter says the second diabetes tablet was started 2 months ago, and his ankles have swollen since.

**Which of the following of his regular medications most likely contributed to this episode?**

- A. amlodipine
- B. metformin
- C. pioglitazone  **(key)**
- D. ramipril
- E. tadalafil

*Explanation:* Of the options, the only diabetes drug besides metformin is pioglitazone, so it is the tablet started 2 months ago, after which his ankles swelled. The ESC guideline notes that thiazolidinediones cause sodium and water retention and does not recommend them in heart failure, because they increase the risk of worsening and admission. Amlodipine causes ankle swelling by dilating arterioles, not by retaining fluid, and it does not cause pulmonary edema. Ramipril lowers afterload and is protective. Metformin does not retain fluid. Tadalafil matters for nitrate safety but does not cause fluid retention.

*Key feature:* Pulmonary edema 2. While stabilizing pulmonary edema, search for the underlying cause, such as MI, volume overload, or anemia.

*Source:* McDonagh TA, Metra M, Adamo M, et al. 2021 ESC Guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of acute and chronic heart failure. Eur Heart J. 2021. https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehab368

#### Q3
*Update:* The ECG shows sinus rhythm with left ventricular hypertrophy and no acute ischemic change. Troponin is below the 99th percentile twice. Echo shows an ejection fraction of 55% with concentric hypertrophy and no valve disease. Pioglitazone has been stopped.

**Which of the following changes to his long-term care best reduces the chance of another episode?**

- A. Add a long-acting oral nitrate
- B. Arrange coronary angiography
- C. Intensify antihypertensive therapy  **(key)**
- D. Start digoxin 0.125 mg PO daily
- E. Start ivabradine 5 mg PO twice daily

*Explanation:* A preserved ejection fraction with concentric hypertrophy and an onset at a BP of 194/108 mmHg point to uncontrolled hypertension as the remaining driver now that pioglitazone has stopped. The ESC guideline advises treating hypertension and other causes in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. A long-acting nitrate cannot be combined with his daily tadalafil. Two normal troponins and no ischemic change do not call for angiography. Digoxin and ivabradine slow the rate but do not treat the cause, and he is in sinus rhythm.

*Key feature:* Pulmonary edema 3. Treat the identified cause of pulmonary edema, for example with reperfusion, diuresis, or transfusion, to prevent worsening.

*Source:* McDonagh TA, Metra M, Adamo M, et al. 2021 ESC Guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of acute and chronic heart failure. Eur Heart J. 2021. https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehab368

### pulmonary-edema-17: Third night-time admission this year

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 77-year-old woman is brought to the emergency department by ambulance with sudden shortness of breath that woke her at 0300. This is her third such episode in 5 months. Each time she recovered within a day on nitroglycerin and a diuretic. An echocardiogram 4 months ago showed an ejection fraction of 60%, normal wall thickness and no valve disease. She has hypertension on amlodipine, hydrochlorothiazide and doxazosin, claudication and a 50 pack-year smoking history. Last month ramipril was started, and her creatinine rose from 98 to 162 umol/L within 2 weeks, so it was stopped. She has crackles at both bases, an epigastric bruit and no leg edema.

- Temperature: 36.6°C oral
- Pulse: 96/minute
- Resp.: 30/minute
- BP: 204/96 mmHg
- O2 sat: 88% on room air

#### Q1
*Update:* She settles over 6 hours with noninvasive ventilation, nitroglycerin and furosemide. Her creatinine today is 141 umol/L.

**Which of the following underlying causes best explains her recurrent episodes?**

- A. Bilateral renal artery stenosis  **(key)**
- B. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
- C. Intermittent complete heart block
- D. Pheochromocytoma
- E. Primary hyperaldosteronism

*Explanation:* Recurrent flash pulmonary edema with a normal ejection fraction, resistant hypertension on three drugs, a 65% rise in creatinine within 2 weeks of ramipril, an abdominal bruit and diffuse atherosclerosis together point to bilateral renal artery stenosis. The ESC guideline lists hypertensive pulmonary edema among the high-risk features of this condition. Her echo showed normal wall thickness, which argues against hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Heart block would give a slow pulse, not 96/minute. Pheochromocytoma and primary aldosteronism do not explain the creatinine rise with an ACE inhibitor.

*Key feature:* Pulmonary edema 2. While stabilizing pulmonary edema, search for the underlying cause, such as MI, volume overload, or anemia.

*Source:* Mazzolai L, Teixido-Tura G, Lanzi S, et al. 2024 ESC Guidelines for the management of peripheral arterial and aortic diseases. Eur Heart J. 2024. https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehae179

#### Q2
**Which of the following investigations is the most appropriate first test to confirm the cause?**

- A. Captopril renal scintigraphy
- B. Plasma aldosterone to renin ratio
- C. Renal artery duplex ultrasound  **(key)**
- D. Selective renal angiography
- E. Urine metanephrines

*Explanation:* The 2024 ESC peripheral arterial and aortic disease guideline recommends duplex ultrasound as the first imaging test for renal artery stenosis, with CT or MR angiography when duplex is inconclusive. Captopril scintigraphy is a functional test, not the first-line imaging the guideline names. Selective angiography is invasive and is kept for when revascularization is planned. An aldosterone to renin ratio and urine metanephrines look for other causes of secondary hypertension that fit her history less well.

*Key feature:* Pulmonary edema 2. While stabilizing pulmonary edema, search for the underlying cause, such as MI, volume overload, or anemia.

*Source:* Mazzolai L, Teixido-Tura G, Lanzi S, et al. 2024 ESC Guidelines for the management of peripheral arterial and aortic diseases. Eur Heart J. 2024. https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehae179

#### Q3
*Update:* Duplex ultrasound shows severe stenosis at the origin of both renal arteries. The kidneys measure 9.8 cm and 9.4 cm.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate plan to prevent further episodes of pulmonary edema?**

- A. Home CPAP for suspected sleep apnea
- B. Referral for renal artery stenting  **(key)**
- C. Restart ramipril at a lower dose
- D. Start a daily loop diuretic
- E. Start spironolactone

*Explanation:* The ESC guideline advises against routine revascularization of renal artery stenosis but says it should be considered after optimal medical therapy when there are high-risk features such as hypertensive pulmonary edema, which she has had three times, and the kidney is still viable. Kidneys longer than 8 cm are one sign of viability, and hers measure over 9 cm. Restarting an ACE inhibitor with bilateral stenosis risks another rise in creatinine. A loop diuretic or spironolactone treats volume but not the cause of these episodes. Home CPAP treats sleep apnea, which she has not been shown to have.

*Key feature:* Pulmonary edema 3. Treat the identified cause of pulmonary edema, for example with reperfusion, diuresis, or transfusion, to prevent worsening.

*Source:* Mazzolai L, Teixido-Tura G, Lanzi S, et al. 2024 ESC Guidelines for the management of peripheral arterial and aortic diseases. Eur Heart J. 2024. https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehae179

### pulmonary-edema-18: Pink sputum at 29 weeks

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 27-year-old woman who is 29 weeks pregnant presents to the emergency department with 2 days of worsening shortness of breath, orthopnea and cough with pink frothy sputum. She moved to Canada from Eritrea 1 year ago. As a child she had monthly penicillin injections for a heart problem. She has had one prenatal visit and takes a prenatal vitamin. She is anxious and speaking in short phrases. The first heart sound is loud and there is a low-pitched diastolic rumble at the apex. There are crackles to the mid zones of both lungs. There is no leg edema and the fundal height is 29 cm. The ECG shows atrial fibrillation at 148/minute.

- Temperature: 37.1°C oral
- Pulse: 148/minute irregular
- Resp.: 32/minute
- BP: 112/68 mmHg
- O2 sat: 89% on room air
- Weight: 70 kg

#### Q1
**Which of the following is the most likely underlying cause of her pulmonary edema?**

- A. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
- B. Peripartum cardiomyopathy
- C. Pre-eclampsia with lung edema
- D. Pulmonary embolism
- E. Rheumatic mitral stenosis  **(key)**

*Explanation:* A loud first heart sound, an apical diastolic rumble and childhood monthly penicillin, which is secondary prophylaxis after rheumatic fever, point to rheumatic mitral stenosis. The rise in blood volume and heart rate in pregnancy, and now atrial fibrillation, shorten diastolic filling and raise left atrial pressure. Peripartum cardiomyopathy appears late in pregnancy or after delivery and does not cause a diastolic rumble. Her BP of 112/68 mmHg argues against pre-eclampsia. Pulmonary embolism does not produce pink frothy sputum with a diastolic murmur.

*Key feature:* Pulmonary edema 2. While stabilizing pulmonary edema, search for the underlying cause, such as MI, volume overload, or anemia.

*Source:* De Backer J, Haugaa KH, Hasselberg NE, et al. 2025 ESC Guidelines for the management of cardiovascular disease and pregnancy. Eur Heart J. 2025. https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehaf193

#### Q2
**Which of the following drugs is most appropriate to slow her ventricular rate?**

- A. adenosine 6 mg IV
- B. amiodarone 150 mg IV
- C. atenolol 50 mg PO
- D. digoxin 0.25 mg IV
- E. metoprolol 5 mg IV  **(key)**

*Explanation:* In mitral stenosis a fast rate shortens diastole and raises left atrial pressure, so slowing the rate is the main treatment. The ESC pregnancy guideline recommends beta-blockers in symptomatic mitral stenosis, and IV metoprolol is the preferred beta-1 selective agent. Digoxin is an alternative when beta-blockers are not enough and works poorly against high sympathetic drive. Atenolol is linked to fetal growth restriction and acts slowly by mouth. Amiodarone can cause fetal thyroid dysfunction and bradycardia, and its routine use in pregnancy is contraindicated. Adenosine only transiently slows atrial fibrillation.

*Key feature:* Pulmonary edema 3. Treat the identified cause of pulmonary edema, for example with reperfusion, diuresis, or transfusion, to prevent worsening.

*Source:* De Backer J, Haugaa KH, Hasselberg NE, et al. 2025 ESC Guidelines for the management of cardiovascular disease and pregnancy. Eur Heart J. 2025. https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehaf193

#### Q3
*Update:* After metoprolol her pulse is 104/minute. Her saturation is 91% on 6 L/minute by nasal prongs and crackles persist.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate additional medication for her pulmonary congestion?**

- A. dobutamine 5 mcg/kg/minute IV
- B. enalapril 5 mg PO
- C. furosemide 40 mg IV  **(key)**
- D. nitroprusside 0.3 mcg/kg/minute IV
- E. spironolactone 25 mg PO

*Explanation:* Crackles and hypoxemia persist after rate control, and the ESC pregnancy guideline recommends a diuretic when congestion persists despite a beta-blocker in mitral stenosis, used with care for placental flow. Enalapril is contraindicated in pregnancy because of fetal kidney toxicity. Nitroprusside risks fetal cyanide toxicity. Spironolactone has antiandrogen effects on a male fetus and is avoided. Dobutamine speeds the heart and shortens diastolic filling, which worsens a fixed mitral obstruction.

*Key feature:* Pulmonary edema 3. Treat the identified cause of pulmonary edema, for example with reperfusion, diuresis, or transfusion, to prevent worsening.

*Source:* De Backer J, Haugaa KH, Hasselberg NE, et al. 2025 ESC Guidelines for the management of cardiovascular disease and pregnancy. Eur Heart J. 2025. https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehaf193

#### Q4
*Update:* Echocardiography shows a mitral valve area of 0.9 cm2 and a dilated left atrium. She remains in atrial fibrillation.

**Which of the following antithrombotic plans is most appropriate for her now?**

- A. apixaban 5 mg PO twice daily
- B. ASA 81 mg PO daily
- C. dabigatran 150 mg PO twice daily
- D. enoxaparin 70 mg SC twice daily  **(key)**
- E. No anticoagulation

*Explanation:* Atrial fibrillation with mitral stenosis and a dilated left atrium carries a high risk of left atrial thrombus, and the ESC pregnancy guideline recommends full therapeutic-dose anticoagulation, with low molecular weight heparin or a vitamin K antagonist. Enoxaparin 1 mg/kg twice daily at 70 kg is 70 mg. DOACs are contraindicated in pregnancy and are not indicated for atrial fibrillation with moderate or severe mitral stenosis. ASA does not prevent cardioembolic stroke adequately. Leaving her without anticoagulation ignores a very high stroke risk.

### pulmonary-edema-19: Warfarin held for a dental extraction

- [ ] Approved  - [ ] Needs change  - [ ] Reject

A 58-year-old man presents to the emergency department with 3 days of worsening shortness of breath, now at rest, and 2 nights of sleeping in a chair. He had a mechanical mitral valve replacement 6 years ago and takes warfarin. He stopped warfarin 10 days ago for a dental extraction and has not restarted it. He has no fever or chest pain. He is pale and speaking in short sentences. The crisp mechanical closing click heard at his last clinic visit is now muffled. There are crackles to the mid zones of both lungs.

- Temperature: 36.8°C oral
- Pulse: 112/minute
- Resp.: 30/minute
- BP: 102/64 mmHg
- O2 sat: 88% on room air

#### Q1
*Update:* His INR is 1.3. Hemoglobin is 138 g/L and white cell count 8.4 x 10^9/L. The ECG shows sinus tachycardia.

**Which of the following is the most likely cause of his pulmonary edema?**

- A. Acute coronary syndrome
- B. Paravalvular leak
- C. Prosthetic valve endocarditis
- D. Prosthetic valve thrombosis  **(key)**
- E. Pulmonary embolism

*Explanation:* A mechanical mitral valve, 10 days without warfarin, an INR of 1.3 and a muffled closing click point to obstructive valve thrombosis. Loss of the normal click means a leaflet is no longer moving freely. He has no fever and a normal white cell count, which makes endocarditis less likely. A paravalvular leak adds a regurgitant murmur but leaves the click crisp. He has no chest pain or ischemic ECG change. Pulmonary embolism does not cause bilateral crackles and orthopnea.

*Key feature:* Pulmonary edema 2. While stabilizing pulmonary edema, search for the underlying cause, such as MI, volume overload, or anemia.

*Source:* Praz F, Borger MA, Lanz J, et al. 2025 ESC/EACTS Guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease. Eur Heart J. 2025.

#### Q2
**Which of the following investigations will best confirm the cause of his decompensation?**

- A. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging
- B. Coronary angiography
- C. CT pulmonary angiography
- D. Radionuclide ventriculography
- E. Transesophageal echocardiography  **(key)**

*Explanation:* The ESC and EACTS valve guideline recommends transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography, with fluoroscopy or CT where needed, when valve thrombosis is suspected. The transesophageal view shows the mitral prosthesis clearly, measures the gradient and separates thrombus from pannus. Magnetic resonance is degraded by the metal valve and is too slow for an unstable patient. Coronary and pulmonary angiography look for causes his findings do not support. Radionuclide imaging measures ejection fraction but not leaflet motion.

*Key feature:* Pulmonary edema 2. While stabilizing pulmonary edema, search for the underlying cause, such as MI, volume overload, or anemia.

*Source:* Praz F, Borger MA, Lanz J, et al. 2025 ESC/EACTS Guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease. Eur Heart J. 2025.

#### Q3
*Update:* Transesophageal echo shows a large thrombus on the mitral prosthesis with one leaflet fixed and a mean gradient of 18 mmHg. Cardiac surgery is available on site.

**Which of the following is the most appropriate definitive treatment for this complication?**

- A. Alteplase systemic infusion
- B. Catheter thrombectomy
- C. Emergency valve surgery  **(key)**
- D. Heparin infusion alone
- E. Warfarin with a higher INR target

*Explanation:* The ESC and EACTS guideline recommends urgent or emergency valve replacement for obstructive mechanical valve thrombosis in a critically ill patient without serious comorbidity. Surgery is on site. Systemic fibrinolysis is reserved for when surgery is not available or carries very high risk, because it brings embolic stroke and bleeding. Heparin alone suits a small nonobstructive thrombus, not a fixed leaflet. A higher INR target will not clear an obstruction that is causing pulmonary edema now. Catheter thrombectomy is not an established treatment for this problem.

*Key feature:* Pulmonary edema 3. Treat the identified cause of pulmonary edema, for example with reperfusion, diuresis, or transfusion, to prevent worsening.

*Source:* Praz F, Borger MA, Lanz J, et al. 2025 ESC/EACTS Guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease. Eur Heart J. 2025.

#### Q4
*Update:* While he waits for the operating room, his BP falls to 78/50 mmHg and his skin is cool and mottled. Lactate is 5.1 mmol/L.

**Which of the following vasoactive infusions is the most appropriate first agent for him?**

- A. dobutamine infusion
- B. dopamine infusion
- C. milrinone infusion
- D. norepinephrine infusion  **(key)**
- E. phenylephrine infusion

*Explanation:* He has cardiogenic shock with a BP of 78/50 mmHg and a lactate of 5.1 mmol/L. The ESC guideline suggests norepinephrine as the vasopressor of choice when perfusion pressure must be restored. Dobutamine and milrinone lower systemic resistance and can drop his pressure further across a fixed obstruction. Dopamine causes more arrhythmias than norepinephrine, and a faster rate shortens diastolic filling across the blocked valve. Phenylephrine raises afterload with no inotropic support and can lower cardiac output.

*Key feature:* Pulmonary edema 4. Watch for impending respiratory failure in pulmonary edema and escalate with ventilatory support or vasoactive drugs.

*Source:* McDonagh TA, Metra M, Adamo M, et al. 2021 ESC Guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of acute and chronic heart failure. Eur Heart J. 2021. https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehab368

### pulmonary-edema-20: Chest ache after moving furniture

A 31-year-old man presents to the emergency department with 4 days of left-sided chest ache and shortness of breath that began while he was moving furniture. He waited because he thought he had strained a muscle. He smokes tobacco and has no medical history. He is speaking in full sentences. Breath sounds are absent over the left chest and the trachea is midline. A chest X-ray shows a complete left pneumothorax with the lung collapsed toward the hilum and no mediastinal shift. A 12F chest drain is inserted and connected to an underwater seal without suction.

- Temperature: 36.8°C oral
- Pulse: 104/minute
- Resp.: 24/minute
- BP: 128/78 mmHg
- O2 sat: 91% on room air

#### Q1
*Update:* Forty minutes later he has repeated coughing and pink frothy sputum. He has not vomited. His saturation is 85% on 6 L/minute. The drain is bubbling and swinging. A repeat X-ray shows the left lung expanded, with new airspace opacity throughout it.

**Which of the following is the most likely cause of his new hypoxemia?**

- A. Aspiration pneumonitis
- B. Hemothorax from the drain
- C. Pulmonary embolism
- D. Re-expansion pulmonary edema  **(key)**
- E. Tension pneumothorax

*Explanation:* Cough, pink frothy sputum and hypoxemia within an hour of draining a lung that had been collapsed for 4 days, with new opacity confined to the re-expanded lung, fit re-expansion pulmonary edema. The BTS statement describes hypoxemia and new alveolar infiltrates after rapid re-expansion, usually within the first hour, and notes the risk when a large pneumothorax is drained. A bubbling, swinging drain and an expanded lung argue against tension pneumothorax. A hemothorax shows pleural fluid, not airspace opacity with frothy sputum. Embolism would not confine new opacity to the drained lung, and he has not vomited to aspirate.

*Key feature:* Pulmonary edema 2. While stabilizing pulmonary edema, search for the underlying cause, such as MI, volume overload, or anemia.

*Source:* Asciak R, Bedawi EO, Bhatnagar R, et al. British Thoracic Society clinical statement on pleural procedures. Thorax. 2023. https://doi.org/10.1136/thorax-2022-219371

#### Q2
**Which of the following is the most appropriate immediate step in his management?**

- A. Apply suction to the drain
- B. Clamp the chest drain
- C. furosemide 40 mg IV
- D. Oxygen with the drain unclamped  **(key)**
- E. Remove the chest drain

*Explanation:* The BTS statement manages re-expansion pulmonary edema with oxygen to target saturations and advises against clamping a bubbling drain, because a continuing air leak can build into a tension pneumothorax. Clamping is the step for a drain placed for an effusion, not for this bubbling pneumothorax drain. Suction speeds re-expansion, and the statement advises against routine suction at the first drainage of a pneumothorax. Removing the drain lets the pneumothorax recur. Diuretics are used by some clinicians but are not evidence based.

*Key feature:* Pulmonary edema 3. Treat the identified cause of pulmonary edema, for example with reperfusion, diuresis, or transfusion, to prevent worsening.
